## the arc of the course

The syllabus moves in four big steps. Seeing them makes each reading easier to place.

1. **The mystery (Part I).** Global governance is hard to see: who rules, and through what? The field is framed as *people with projects*, with a history of ideas you can chart ([[kennedy-chart]], [[berman-modernism]]).
2. **The architecture (Part II, Topics 2–9).** The standard toolkit of public international law: **norms** (custom, sources, hierarchy), **entities** (states, individuals), **powers** (jurisdiction, territory), and how the machine is **mobilised** (state responsibility, countermeasures). Read these doctrinally *and* ask what each doctrine assumes about sovereignty.
3. **The architecture in history (Part III, Topics 10–12).** The same toolkit, historicised: pre-modern naturalism, interwar modernism, then post-1945 positivism, policy-science and the return to norms. The syllabus explicitly sends you *back* to Topic 4 cases here: reading them twice is the point.
4. **New thinking (Parts IV–IX, Topics 13–22).** How later scholarship reimagines law's role through adjudication, consciousness (political economy, human rights), administration, legislation/private ordering, theories of the whole (fragmentation, hierarchy, the local), and finally war as a legal institution.

## topics 1–9 in one page: the architecture and its seams

**The frame (Topic 1).** The casebook tells a smooth progress story: natural law, then positivism, then institutions ([[damrosch-intro]]). Kennedy's chart ([[kennedy-chart]]) and Berman ([[berman-modernism]]) replace it with *professional sensibilities* that shift with each era's trauma. Each doctrine below was someone's project.

**Norms (Topics 2–3).** The doctrine of sources promised a positive science of state will ([[schachter-custom]]), but every element is soft:
- custom is found by courts choosing whose practice counts ([[paquete-habana]] vs [[lotus]]) or treated as a process of claims ([[mcdougal-custom]]);
- general principles rest on implied consensus ([[schachter-general-principles]]);
- jus cogens is circular and gendered ([[ilc-fragmentation-jus-cogens]], [[charlesworth-chinkin-jus-cogens]]);
- the law/non-law line moves: a press conference binds ([[nuclear-tests]]), a negotiated accord doesn't ([[schachter-twilight]]), and 'changed circumstances' lets the powerful exit ([[lissitzyn-load-line]]).

The Court twice escapes the hardest nuclear question ([[nuclear-tests]], [[nuclear-weapons-ao]]).

**Entities (Topics 4–6).** Statehood looks factual ([[montevideo]]) but was a Latin American anti-imperial achievement ([[becker-lorca-mestizo]]), is narrated into and out of existence ([[nyt-putin-ukraine]]), and is 'constrained' in postcolonial small states ([[robinson-caribbean]]). Sovereignty can be:
- absolute ([[schooner-exchange]]);
- divisible under empire ([[maine-kathiawar]]);
- 'pure fact' ([[american-banana]], [[salimoff]]);
- private ([[cohen-property-sovereignty]]);
- a 'social function' ([[corfu-1949]], Álvarez);
- layered ([[knop-feminism-sovereignty]]).

The individual enters through nationality ([[nottebohm]]) and criminal responsibility, but trials are caught between impunity and show trial ([[koskenniemi-impunity]]), contested by sovereigntists ([[bolton-icc-2018]]) and by critics of carceral feminism ([[engle-grip]]) and of legalism over Gaza ([[krever-scurrying]], [[miller-times-violence]], [[drumbl-smouldering]]).

**Powers (Topics 7–8).** Jurisdiction moved from strict territoriality ([[american-banana]]) to effects and reasonableness ([[restatement-402-404]]) to the reach of finance ([[bank-nova-scotia]], [[sauerbrey-europe]]). Its language of 'national interest' hides normative choices ([[malley-manas-nix]]), and it could instead track communities ([[berman-globalization-jurisdiction]]). Territory moved from effective display among empires ([[island-of-palmas]]), to decolonisation's two answers (self-determination in [[western-sahara]], frozen colonial lines in [[burkina-mali]]), to a Charter-era ban on conquest ([[vidmar-greenland]]). Internationalised territory is a legal fantasy that has to make room for other fantasies ([[berman-jerusalem]]).

**Mobilisation (Topic 9).** Without a central enforcer, the system runs on responsibility and *self-help* ([[ilc-arsiwa]]). Proportionality is the only brake, applied strictly in [[gabcikovo]] and loosely in [[air-services]], and it gets stretched into terrorism ([[lillich-paxman]]) and cyberwar ([[nyt-cyber-iran]]).

**Through-lines to use in papers:** fact vs form (who benefits when effectiveness beats title?), who decides (court, Council, national judge, or the injured state alone?), and the public/private split ([[cohen-property-sovereignty]], [[charlesworth-chinkin-jus-cogens]], [[barcelona-traction]]).

## questions to carry through every reading

- **What is the author's project?** What do they want international law to *do*?
- **Where do they draw the law / non-law line?** ([[law-nonlaw]])
- **What picture of sovereignty does this assume?** Absolute, relative, divided, constructed? ([[sovereignty]])
- **Who is inside and who is outside?** Civilised/uncivilised, public/private, state/market, male/female. ([[colonialism]], [[public-private]], [[feminist-critique]])
- **Which period's style of thought is this?** Pre-modern, modernist (1914–45), post-1945, or "new thinking"? Use the chart.
- **What are the dark sides?** What does the project cost, and for whom? ([[dark-sides]])
