# Lecture 4: Statehood and recognition; Schooner Exchange

## what Kennedy stressed

- 'Forget whether an international rule is binding; ask **in what scenarios the rule is valid**.'
- After WWII the focus shifts from specific norms to **how the system works**. The pre-war Court was dominated by sources questions; the post-war Court by procedure and jurisdiction.
- Norms are **upgraded and downgraded at the same time**: General Assembly resolutions express 'collective consciousness', non-binding deals gain force, while treaties are applied pragmatically.
- The **'international community'** appears as a new actor without sovereignty: diplomats, lawyers and democratic publics, held together by a shared sensibility.
- **Recognition as a 2×2:** political choice or rule-based, crossed with constitutive or declaratory ([[montevideo]], [[recognition]]). In practice lawyers advise and presidents decide on interest. 'Could you rethink that question?' is the client–lawyer dynamic.
- **Statehood is a bundle, not a binary:** effective here but not there, for these purposes, for these actors. In [[salimoff]] the political branch performs the legal act and the court the pragmatic one ('I'll take the oil'). Taiwan meets Montevideo but is unrecognised; its strategy runs through courts, contracts, supply chains and small states. Google Maps is a sovereignty actor.
- **When does a non-binding statement become binding?** There is no technical threshold, only a sociological assessment in context (Ukraine and Russia, the Patriot 'handshake'). 'The ICJ is just another participant in the conversation.'
- [[schooner-exchange]]: Marshall resolves the property question by deciding not to resolve it: public vs private, 'exploring an unbeaten path'.

### Course Arc and Where We Are

- Course has traced law and global affairs from late 19th century through post-WWII
- Two competing visions established early:
- Law as problem-solving: institutional presence inventing techniques to resolve conflicts
- Law as norms: validated through courts and adjudication (Article 38 as the mechanism)
- Most innovations since 1945 have been attempts to make the normative system work better

### The Norm Problem: From Positivism to Process

- Article 38 promised an objective system for validating norms; proved harder than expected
- Stringent standards (Takumi Wada approach) meant very few norms could be found
- Responses to the norm scarcity problem:
- Codification via treaty: workable but consent problems persist
- Scholarly codification (ILC, treatises): different views produced different “rules”
- McDougallite turn: abandon the search for definitive answers; work the interactive process
- McDougall shift: from court or scholar deciding once, to claims made in real diplomatic time
- No final rulebook; what emerges is what has worked in context
- Law becomes a lexicon of persuasion arguments, effective partly through sanctions

### Upgrading and Downgrading: Soft Law and Treaty Flexibility

- Norm hierarchy became a spectrum, not binary yes/no
- Strong norms, weak norms, soft law (persuasive but not strictly binding)
- General Assembly resolutions: not legislation, but express collective consciousness
- Non-binding instruments upgraded: unilateral declarations, abstract principles, handshake agreements
- Treaties simultaneously downgraded:
- Cardozo in Techt v. Hughes: apply treaties pragmatically, proposition by proposition
- Change of circumstance, unequal bargaining power as grounds to escape
- Jus cogens: super-principles above treaty law; more symbolic than operative in practice
- Charlesworth and Chinkin article on gendered jus cogens as example

### The International Community as a New Actor

- Shift from states-in-a-void to an “international community” without sovereignty
- Diplomats, international lawyers, publics in democracies all potentially part of it
- Oriented by shared sensibility about what arguments are good or bad
- Publics matter: citizens in democracies can vote differently based on international law violations
- Post-WWII focus moves from specific norms to how the system works
- Pre-WWII ICJ: dominated by sources-of-law questions
- Post-WWII ICJ: dominated by procedural and jurisdictional questions

### Statehood: Rules vs. Recognition Process

- Montevideo criteria (territory, people, government, capacity for foreign relations): declaratory view
- Statehood achieved organically through consolidation of monopoly of force
- Recognition merely declares what already exists
- Constitutive view: statehood only real once recognized by others
- Four resulting positions on recognition:
- Political choice + constitutive (I recognize you, therefore you exist)
- Political choice + declaratory (I won’t recognize you, but you still exist)
- Rule-based + declaratory (clerks apply criteria, declare the fact)
- Rule-based + constitutive (rules determine who counts, recognition makes it real)
- In practice: recognition is a policy instrument
- Lawyers advise; presidents decide based on interest (oil, weapons deals, alliances)
- “Could you rethink that question?” is the client-lawyer dynamic in action

### Statehood as a Bundle, Not a Binary

- Sovereignty/statehood is a matter of more or less, not yes or no
- Effective here but not there; for these purposes but not those; for these actors but not others
- Salimoff case: State Department withholds de jure recognition of Soviet government
- Courts independently recognize it as de facto government
- Odd reversal: political branch does the legal act; legal branch does the pragmatic one
- Soviet nationalization: courts respect property transfers even without State Department recognition
- “I’ll take the oil” logic: sovereignty achieved through subordinated decisions across locations
- Taiwan example: full Montevideo criteria met, yet unrecognized by most states
- Campaign strategy: get courts to recognize contracts, build supply-chain leverage, pay off small states
- Google Maps as a sovereignty actor: corporate map decisions may matter more than many states’ positions

### Non-Binding Agreements and the Russia-Ukraine Question

- Student question: when do non-binding statements become binding?
- Post-1945 answer: no technical threshold; sociological assessment required in context
- Ukraine claims binding; Russia claims non-binding; persuasiveness determines effect
- Nuclear Tests case (France): unilateral statement can become binding, but unlikely to persuade in current war
- Patriot missile handshake example:
- Constituencies mobilize behind “already decided” argument
- Opponents argue it was never binding
- Outcome is product of the unfolding political-legal conversation, not a rule
- Student legal argument: states have duty not to treat unlawful acts of another state as lawful
- Extension: obligation to prevent domestic corporations (e.g. Google) from doing the same
- Diagnosed as soft law argument rooted in sovereignty interpretation; persuasive but not decisive
- ICJ is “just another participant in the conversation”

### Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon (1812): Sovereignty Meets Property

- Facts: American’s private vessel seized by Napoleon, later spotted in Philadelphia flying French flag; owner sues for return
- Marshall’s holding: implied consent to sovereign immunity
- Port assumed open to friendly foreign military vessels absent explicit decree
- US sovereign could have excluded the ship; did not; therefore immunity implied
- Key tension: property rights vs. sovereign immunity vs. US territorial sovereignty
- Marshall resolves by classifying the ship as a public vessel, not a property dispute
- Resolves the property question by deciding not to resolve it
- Odd judicial restraint: Marshall, usually expansive on judicial power, defers to executive on foreign policy
- Political backdrop (war with Britain) likely decisive, but legal argument does real work
- Public/private distinction: private property claims don’t travel the same way sovereign rights do
- Method: no Roman law principles invoked; reasoning from analogous situations and general principles
- “Exploring an unbeaten path” acknowledged by Marshall himself

- M. Salimoff & Co. v. Standard Oil Co. of New York (1933), pp. 16–17 Facts: The Soviet government confiscated Russian oilfields and sold the extracted oil to American companies. The former owners, Russian nationals, sued those companies. They argued that, because the United States did not recognise the Soviet government, its confiscation decrees had no legal effect. Decision: The New York Court of Appeals rejected the claims. The Soviet government exercised effective authority within Russia. Its laws governing Russian territory and Russian nationals could therefore have legal consequences despite American diplomatic non-recognition. Reasoning: Courts could acknowledge the existence and domestic authority of a government without formally recognising it diplomatically. Treating the Soviet government as merely a gang of thieves would ignore political reality. Key lesson: Diplomatic non-recognition does not automatically invalidate everything an effective government does within its territory. Recognition and actual governmental authority are distinct.
- The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon (1812), pp. 21–26 Facts: American owners claimed that France had unlawfully seized their merchant ship and converted it into a French warship. When the vessel entered Philadelphia, they sought to recover it through an American court. Question: Could an American court exercise jurisdiction over a foreign sovereign’s warship in an American port? Decision: The US Supreme Court held that the ship was immune from American judicial jurisdiction in these circumstances. The owners’ action was dismissed. Reasoning: A state generally has exclusive jurisdiction within its territory, but it can consent to exceptions. By admitting a friendly foreign warship, the United States had implicitly accepted that the vessel would retain immunity. Marshall connected this to sovereign equality and the practical requirements of relations between states. Key lesson: Territorial sovereignty can accommodate sovereign immunity through implied consent. The judgment distinguishes public warships from ordinary private individuals and merchant vessels, which generally remain subject to local law. It resolves jurisdiction rather than determining whether the original seizure was lawful.
- The Antelope (1825), pp. 27–35 Facts: An American revenue cutter intercepted a ship carrying more than 280 enslaved Africans. They had been taken from several vessels by a privateer largely crewed by Americans. Spanish and Portuguese representatives demanded their return as the property of their nationals; the United States argued for their freedom. Question: Did the slave trade violate international law, allowing American courts to reject foreign ownership claims? Decision: The Supreme Court held that, although the trade violated natural justice, it was not prohibited by the international law the Court understood to apply at that time. American abolition legislation could not itself bind other states. Reasoning: Marshall grounded international law in state practice and consent. Because states had historically permitted the trade, its prohibition by some countries did not automatically make it unlawful for all. Sovereign equality prevented one country from imposing its legislation on another. Actual outcome: The Spanish claim was partly upheld, with the number used to calculate restitution reduced. The Court was divided on whether restitution to the Spanish claimants was required in these circumstances, so that part of the lower court’s ruling stood. The Portuguese claim failed for lack of proof of ownership. The remaining Africans were placed at the disposal of the United States under its laws. Key lesson: The case exposes the tension between moral principle and international law based on state practice and consent. Here, respect for sovereignty helped sustain claims to human ownership.
- American Banana Co. v. United Fruit Co. (1909), pp. 42–45 Facts: American Banana alleged that United Fruit sought to eliminate it as a competitor by inducing Costa Rican officials to seize its plantation and obstruct its railway. The plantation’s territorial status was disputed, but Costa Rica exercised actual control. American Banana sued under US antitrust legislation. Question: Could American antitrust law provide a remedy for conduct abroad involving acts of a foreign government? Decision: The Supreme Court dismissed the claim. Reasoning: Holmes treated legislation as presumptively territorial. The Sherman Act did not cover the foreign conduct alleged in this case. He also reasoned that American courts could not condemn Costa Rica’s sovereign seizure within territory under its effective control. Alleging that United Fruit had persuaded Costa Rica to act did not overcome those obstacles. Key lesson: Territorial limits on domestic legislation and judicial deference to foreign sovereign acts can prevent remedies against private companies. This is the historical position taken in the judgment, rather than a complete statement of present-day antitrust jurisdiction.
- Corfu Channel: United Kingdom v. Albania (1949), p. 53 What the pack includes: Only a short extract from Judge Álvarez’s individual opinion, rather than the case’s facts and the Court’s full judgment. Argument: Sovereignty remains essential, but it cannot mean unlimited freedom from external obligations. It gives states rights and imposes duties. International interdependence and the general interest require states to comply with rules that do not necessarily depend on their individual consent. Key lesson: Sovereignty operates within an international legal order. Álvarez presents it as a social function carrying responsibilities. This challenges the strongly consent-based conception illustrated by The Antelope. His formulation should be attributed to his individual opinion.
- The Kathiawar States and Sovereignty (1864), pp. 36–41 Type of material: A colonial legal memorandum, not a judicial judgment. Problem: Were the Kathiawar states in India British territory, automatically subject to British legislation, or did their rulers retain sovereignty? Position taken: The states retained some sovereign powers, including immunity from the automatic application of British laws, limited judicial authority and, in some cases, coinage. Britain nevertheless possessed extensive powers of intervention. Reasoning: Sovereignty was understood as a collection of powers that could be divided between different authorities. The allocation depended on actual relations and practice. Earlier British disclaimers of complete sovereignty also mattered. Key lesson: Sovereignty can be divisible without amounting to full independence. The memorandum also illustrates how recognising limited local sovereignty could coexist with justifying extensive colonial control.

### Attorney-General for Bermuda v. Ferguson (2022), p. 14

The Privy Council held that Bermuda’s Constitution did not confer a right to same-sex marriage. The chapter uses this to explore how local constitutional politics remain connected to judicial authority in London.

### Day v. Governor of the Cayman Islands (2022), p. 14

The Privy Council likewise found no constitutional right to same-sex marriage in the Cayman Islands. It illustrates the relationship between local political identity, rights disputes and continuing British constitutional authority.

### Bahamian citizenship appeal (2023), pp. 14–15

The Privy Council held that citizenship through a Bahamian “parent”, for people born in the Bahamas, included children born outside marriage to Bahamian fathers and non-Bahamian mothers. The extract does not supply the case name.

### The Amedie and The Fortuna, discussed on p. 29

British decisions refusing restitution in slave-trading cases. As presented in The Antelope, the claimant’s own national law prohibiting the trade was central to the reasoning.

### The Diana, discussed on p. 29

A Swedish slave-trading vessel and its human cargo were ordered restored because Swedish law permitted the trade. British opposition to slavery did not itself determine the foreign claimant’s rights.

### Le Louis, discussed on p. 30

A French slave-trading vessel was ordered restored. The judgment rejected a general peacetime right to search foreign vessels without treaty permission and refused to equate slave trading with piracy.
