# Lecture 2: Norms: Berman, Article 38, Paquete Habana, Lotus, McDougal

## what Kennedy stressed

- **Two roads from the early 1900s.** One is institutional problem-solving (Geneva, the League, Berman's plebiscites and minority treaties: [[berman-modernism]]). The other is norms made to work through a court and adjudication (The Hague: the PCIJ and [[icj-art38]]).
- Article 38 gives the court **'rules about rules'** so it can look neutral and scientific. But custom is almost impossible to verify ([[paquete-habana]], [[schachter-custom]]). That is 'the dark side' of the positivist project.
- **Validity → persuasion and effectiveness.** Article 38 survives as a vocabulary in everyone's 'thought bubble', not as a scientific instrument ([[mcdougal-custom]]).
- [[lotus]]: when rules are scarce, **the burden of proof decides**. The Court deduces from the *nature of sovereignty* (territory first, people second), which opens a second channel of argument.
- The colonial blind spot runs through both the traditional model and the interwar innovations ([[colonialism]]).

### Traditional vs. Modern International Law

- Traditional model: sovereign states as triangles, each with internal legal order, relating horizontally through consent
- No super-sovereign above; rules derived from consensual agreement between states
- Displaced earlier naturalism, where norms pre-existed states and derived from reason/justice
- WWI shattered confidence in the traditional model among European legal elites
- Two reform branches emerged in the interwar period:
- Geneva branch: League of Nations, institutional problem-solving, associated with Berman’s analysis
- The Hague branch: adjudication, arbitration, Permanent Court of International Justice

### Berman’s Four Modernist Ideas

- Critique of representation: law as a mirror of state consent misrepresents reality
- Nations (not states) are the real political units; nationalism as a force doesn’t map onto state boundaries
- Wilsonian self-determination: align triangles with nations, but Berman’s subjects reject this as unworkable
- Two alternative techniques for managing nationalism without redrawing all borders:
- Plebiscites: ask populations which state they want to join, managed by international institutions
- Minority protection: guarantee language, schools, religion for ethnic minorities within existing states (e.g., German speakers in Czechoslovakia)
- Contradictory elements: no single coherent principle; different techniques applied case by case
- New professional role: international lawyers as activist problem-solvers, not rule-appliers
- Law becomes a portfolio of management techniques, not a normative framework

### Article 38 and the Doctrine of Sources

- Article 38 of the PCIJ/ICJ statute: the “intellectual instrument of objective legal validation”
- Sources: treaties, custom (practice accepted as law), general principles of civilized nations
- Positivist in orientation: validity of a rule is a matter of verifiable fact, not morality
- Schachter (1991) retrospective: the scientific validity model was “more myth than reality”
- In practice, courts rarely identified customary norms through detailed state practice analysis
- 2017 study: not one ICJ/PCIJ case positively identified custom via rigorous state practice fact-finding
- State practice analysis was used mainly to establish the absence of a norm

### The Problem of Custom: Paquete Habana (1900)

- Facts: US Navy seized Cuban fishing vessels during blockade; question of whether customary immunity applied
- Established proposition: international law is part of US law, ascertainable and administered by courts
- Justice Gray’s clerks conducted exhaustive historical research into British, French, Spanish practice
- Found a rule: fishing vessels immune from seizure unless provisioning enemy troops
- But the method is fragile: requires uniform, consistent state practice, which is almost never available
- Codification response: Harvard Draft (Manley Hudson) attempted to systematize customary rules
- ILC’s 2018 effort still couldn’t resolve core questions: what counts as practice, how much evidence, opinio juris thresholds

### From Validity to Persuasion and Effectiveness

- Shift across the 20th century: from rule validity to persuasiveness and effectiveness
- Persuasion: convincing others a rule exists and applies
- Effectiveness: achieving compliance regardless of persuasion, through reputational cost, sanctions, naval presence
- Article 38 survives as advocacy vocabulary, not scientific instrument
- Advocacy practice: choose most persuasive state practice first (the disputing state itself, then powerful trading partners, then broad coalitions)
- Custom doesn’t die as a category; it thrives as a tool for making claims and demanding responses
- Holmes parallel: the scope of a right is the scope of its remedy (bad man theory applied internationally)

### McDougal’s Process Theory of Custom

- Custom reframed as “continuous interaction, continuous demand and response”
- States unilaterally make claims; other decision-makers weigh and accept or reject them
- Courts are peripheral; the real process is decentralized diplomatic advocacy
- Emphasizes reasonableness, human process values, common interest
- “World constitutive process of authoritative decision”: law, diplomacy, ethics, and power are inseparable
- Isolating the legal element from the political or military is a “fool’s errand”
- Context: McDougal was a Cold War strategist; his framework served US hegemonic management
- Favored legality of hydrogen bomb testing in open seas; not a neutral framework

### The Lotus Case: Sovereignty as Legal Principle

- Facts: French steamer Lotus collides with Turkish vessel on the high seas; Turkish sailors die; France’s officer Demons arrested and tried in Turkey
- Political backdrop: Turkey as newly modernized state asserting equal sovereignty, reversing Ottoman-era capitulations
- Core dispute: who bears the burden of proof?
- France: Turkey must show a positive rule granting it jurisdiction
- Turkey: sovereignty permits action until a rule prohibits it
- Court decided for Turkey: sovereignty is territorial first; restrictions on sovereignty cannot be presumed
- Turkish ship assimilated to Turkish territory; effects of the collision occurred “on Turkish soil”
- Nationality principle (France’s claim over its citizen/ship) subordinated to territoriality
- Method: court derived the rule from the nature of sovereignty itself, not from Article 38 sources
- No treaty cited, no custom analysis; principles deduced from the concept of sovereignty
- 6 dissenting judges: the outcome was genuinely contested
- Significance: opens a second channel of legal argument alongside Article 38
- Advocates can argue the meaning and nature of sovereignty, not just specific rules
- Both sides can claim sovereignty; the principle expands rather than resolves disputes
- Aftermath: immediately generated counter-arguments that sovereignty implies duties, not just rights
- Duties of peaceful cooperation, diplomatic engagement, etc., deduced from the social nature of a multi-sovereign world

### Key Tensions and Takeaways

- Scientific validity model vs. advocacy/process model: Article 38 remains the vocabulary but not the mechanism
- Positivism’s internal problem: demanding verifiable consent-based rules in a world where most practice was never codified
- Sovereignty as double-edged: both France and Turkey could invoke it; the Lotus method multiplies claims rather than settling them
- Colonial blind spot in both branches: traditional model and interwar innovations were North Atlantic projects; colonial reorganization of most of the world proceeded on entirely different legal logic
