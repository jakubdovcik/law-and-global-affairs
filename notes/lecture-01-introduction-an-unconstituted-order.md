# Lecture 1: Introduction: an unconstituted order

## what Kennedy stressed

- International reality is **not a constituted order**: not coherent, unified or rational, and it looks different from different perspectives.
- International law is **not always on the side of peace and order**, and it is not its own thing: it sits inside economic and political order. Keep this in mind against the casebook's progress story ([[damrosch-intro]], [[kennedy-chart]]).
- Periods: pre-1648 (religion, empire, war), 1648–1918 (positivism, a 'period of thinking'), 1918–45 (institutions, a 'period of problem solving'), then 1945–89.
- Every entity that wants to be a party to international law still aims to be a **sovereign**. The imperfect model stays relevant.

### First reading (Casebook of International law)

### A constant tension between the historical and modern perspectives on international law

- ‘Traditionally, international law was seen as a law of the international community of states’
- States are the conceptual foundational parts to international law (as opposed to the citizens)

### Traditional idea of international law

- Parallel institutions between states with parallel institutions
- A residual notion is that some things are legal and some are political (like entities (like Palestine) vs states (Israel))

### Pre-1648

- Religion, empire, war

### 1648 to 1918

- Inclusion of philosophy (mainly natural law ideas) gave birth to positivism in international law (there is nothing above the relations between sovereign nations)
- ‘Period of thinking’

### 1918-1945

- Institutions like League of Nations, permanent court of international law (PCIS)
- Period of problem solving

### 1945-1989

The imperfect model of international affairs remains relevant - every entity trying to be a party of international law aims to be a sovereign power

### International law as a problem solving entity

- Does not represent intra-sovereign bargains
- Understood to be imperfect but useful
- Started to be such after 1918
