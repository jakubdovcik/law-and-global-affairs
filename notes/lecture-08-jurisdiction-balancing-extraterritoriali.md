# Lecture 8: Jurisdiction: balancing, extraterritoriality, the state as jurisdiction

## what Kennedy stressed

- The course triad: **norms – processes – substance**. Validity gives way to persuasion and effectiveness; pragmatism means balancing, proportionality and reasonableness.
- **'Private rights travel, public international law does not.' 'The state is the power it exercises.'**
- Norms shift from doctrines or abstract facts to **motives in interaction**; states play to several audiences. Diplomatic protection is political and legal at once.
- **Jurisdiction is both possessed and asserted.** No overarching rule allocates it, and state power is legally dispersed. The post-1970 innovation is weighing and balancing ([[restatement-402-404]]): expansive bases, then a reasonableness test that every state applies to itself, with no one to enforce it.
- [[bank-nova-scotia]]: the balancing was 'ridiculously subjective'. The US interest was framed as 'the financial integrity of the republic', the Bahamian one as mere privacy, and it could easily have been reversed. By 1982 'where it happened' was too indeterminate to decide anything.
- **Three channels of extraterritoriality:**
  - affirmative assertion (sanctions, antitrust, FCPA). The FCPA was made to stick through World Bank debarment and built a white-collar practice industry in India;
  - unintended impact;
  - private rights travelling.
  - Sanctions work through the dollar's infrastructure ([[sauerbrey-europe]]: Instex); blocking statutes are the counter-move.
- **The 1990 HLR Note** ([[malley-manas-nix]]):
  - a big-country bias;
  - a substantive bias: commercial rights travel, social legislation doesn't, echoing [[antelope]];
  - only interests framed as *national* can enter the balance. The proposed fix risks 'super-imperialism'.
- **The flip:** jurisdictional decisions *define* the state, 'an open-ended constellation of authoritative exercises of jurisdiction'. Burundi is not Brazil. The USSR had nuclear weapons but little legal reach; Belt and Road exports legal compliance with its loans. [[berman-globalization-jurisdiction]] offers a cosmopolitan version.
- 'By 2026 the hopeful post-Cold War moment has passed.' In crises lawyers revert to traditional doctrine: **'the full historical stack persists'**.

### The Law-Politics Tension in International Affairs

- Core question: are international norms political or legal? Process or recognition?
- Constant interplay between two modes: determining things politically vs. applying legal rules
- This tension sharpens some distinctions and softens others
- Key shift: from understanding norms as doctrines or abstract facts to understanding them as motives in interaction

### Evolution of the Interstate Process

- McDougall’s early view: states are the primary actors, bringing claims against each other
- Later expansion: many more actors involved, states play to multiple audiences
- NGOs, corporations, lawyers all make legal-style arguments
- By the 1970s, a much broader socio-legal interaction had emerged
- Adjudicative layer added: international courts deducing norms from sovereignty
- Barcelona Traction, Nottebohm: obligations around nationality and diplomatic protection
- Diplomatic protection is simultaneously political and legal

### Jurisdiction as Concept

- Jurisdiction: authority to affect legal interests; something both possessed and asserted
- Key tension: having jurisdiction vs. making it effective
- Persuasion vs. effectiveness as two distinct routes
- No overarching rule assigns jurisdiction; each entity determines its own
- Power is distributed: different institutions exercise different forms of jurisdiction
- State power is legally dispersed, not centralized in a single sovereign act

### The Weighing and Balancing Framework

- Post-1970 innovation: replace rigid territorial rules with multi-factor reasonableness tests
- US version: weighing and balancing (Third Restatement, §§402-404)
- Elsewhere: proportionality, reasonableness, appropriateness
- §402 bases for jurisdiction (expansive, hard to fall outside):
- Conduct partly or substantially in territory
- Status of persons and things
- Effects intended or felt in territory
- Activities of nationals abroad
- Conduct affecting state security
- §403: reasonableness test applied to whether to exercise jurisdiction
- All policy considerations folded in; no institution enforces the rule
- Everyone is expected to self-apply it

### Bank of Nova Scotia: Balancing in Practice

- US sought documents held in the Bahamas via Miami branch of a Canadian bank
- Bank resisted, citing Bahamian bank secrecy law and Canadian law
- Court ruled US grand jury interest (tax enforcement, financial integrity) outweighed Bahamian secrecy interest
- Critique raised in discussion:
- Balancing was “ridiculously subjective”
- Court framed US interest as “financial integrity of the republic,” Bahamas interest as mere banking privacy
- Could easily be reversed: Bahamas had a foundational economic policy; US had one tax case
- Territoriality argument: court sidestepped it by saying disclosure occurred in the US
- Contrast with earlier cases (Schooner, American Banana) where territorial location was decisive
- By 1982, “where it happened” was too indeterminate to resolve transnational disputes

### Extraterritorial Jurisdiction in Practice

- Three main channels:
- Affirmative extraterritorial assertion (e.g., US sanctions, antitrust, FCPA)
- Unintended extraterritorial impact (e.g., weak labor or environmental standards affecting neighbors)
- Private rights traveling across borders (property, contract, investment protections)
- FCPA as a case study in effective extraterritorial reach:
- US criminalized bribery of foreign officials, then made it stick globally
- World Bank debarment used as enforcement lever for large construction contractors
- Created an entire white-collar practice industry in countries like India
- India had its own Prevention of Corruption Act, but it was never enforced
- Sanctions as jurisdictional tool:
- Effective because global financial actors need access to US financial infrastructure
- Europeans attempted workarounds (INSTEX for Iran) with limited success
- Blocking statutes (like bank secrecy laws) are the counterpart mechanism

### Hidden Biases in the Balancing Framework (Maier/Trimble critique, 1990)

- Two structural biases identified beyond simple self-interest:
- Big-country bias: small states more often the target of extraterritorial exercises
- Substantive bias: private/economic rights travel; social legislation does not
- Antitrust, securities, corruption law enforced globally
- Anti-discrimination law treated as impinging on foreign sovereignty
- Echoes Antelope: property rights trumped US liberty policy even then
- Only interests framed as national interests can enter the balance
- Transnational labor, global social rights cannot be weighed directly
- Must be recast as a national interest to be cognizable
- Proposed fix: recast national interest as an arena of competing claims
- Allow courts to weigh transnational interests explicitly
- Risk flagged: could become a form of super-imperialism

### Rethinking the State Through Jurisdiction

- Classic flip proposed: the state does not define its jurisdiction; jurisdictional decisions define the state
- “The state is an open-ended constellation of authoritative exercises of jurisdiction”
- No permanent inside or outside; no fixed legal interest
- All states are permeable; foreign property rights already effective within territory
- Parallel to norm theory: effective norm = valid norm; effective jurisdiction = the state
- Practical implication: Burundi and Brazil, China and the US are not legally equivalent
- Power is the ability to exercise jurisdiction effectively
- USSR had nuclear weapons but limited extraterritorial legal reach; Belt and Road brought Chinese legal compliance along with loans
- Paul Schiff Berman (2002): cosmopolitan conception
- Community defined by networks of social relations, not state borders
- Non-state actors (corporations, individuals, even gangs) assert jurisdiction
- Non-enforced jurisdictional assertions still have normative effects
- By 2026: the hopeful post-Cold War moment has passed
- In crises, international lawyers revert to traditional doctrinal arguments

- All the old ideas remain live alongside the new ones; the full historical stack persists

### Topic 7 packet: jurisdiction at the national level

The packet moves in a clear arc. It starts with the doctrinal vocabulary (casebook and Restatement), shows that doctrine applied in a hard case (Bank of Nova Scotia), shows the same power operating through finance (the Instex op-ed), and closes with two academic critiques that treat jurisdiction as a way states construct themselves (the 1990 Harvard Law Review Note and Berman 2002). Several readings are excerpted, marked by asterisks, and parts of the Berman scan are cropped and unreadable, so some arguments below are reconstructed from the surviving text.

### 1. Casebook opener (Chapter 11, "Jurisdiction at the National Level", p. 725)

I'm guessing this is Damrosch and Murphy, International Law: Cases and Materials (about 70% confident). The page separates three forms of jurisdiction and adopts the Restatement (Fourth) terms, since each power can be exercised by more than one branch.

Jurisdiction to prescribe (formerly "legislative") is the power to make rules of general import, whether by legislatures, agencies or courts.

Jurisdiction to adjudicate (formerly "judicial") is the power to apply law to persons or things through courts or administrative tribunals (§ 401(b)).

Jurisdiction to enforce (formerly "executive") is the power to compel compliance (§ 401(c)).

The book treats the old and new terms as interchangeable.

### 2. Restatement (Third) of Foreign Relations Law, §§ 402–404

Section 402 sets out the bases of prescriptive jurisdiction:

- Territorial: conduct wholly or substantially within the territory, plus persons and things present there.
- Effects: conduct abroad with substantial intended or actual effect inside.
- Nationality: a state's nationals wherever they are.
- Protective: conduct by foreigners abroad directed against the state's security or a limited class of state interests.

Section 404 adds universal jurisdiction over offences of universal concern, such as piracy, the slave trade, aircraft hijacking, genocide, war crimes and "perhaps" some terrorism. It applies even when no § 402 link exists.

Section 403 is the core of the Third Restatement. A § 402 basis is necessary but insufficient, because jurisdiction must also be reasonable. Reasonableness is assessed against eight factors:

- (a) the activity's territorial link
- (b) connections of nationality, residence or economic activity
- (c) the character and importance of the regulation and how widely it is accepted
- (d) justified expectations
- (e) importance to the international system
- (f) consistency with international tradition
- (g) other states' interests
- (h) likelihood of conflict

Section 403(3) adds a conflict rule. Where both states could reasonably regulate but their commands clash, each must weigh both interests and defer if the other state's interest is "clearly greater".

Context the packet omits: the Restatement (Fourth) of 2018 dropped § 403 reasonableness as a rule of customary international law. Its reporters found too little state practice to support it. International law now requires only a "genuine connection" (§ 407), and reasonableness survives as US domestic "prescriptive comity" (§ 405). I'm about 85% confident in that summary. It matters because the Note and Berman are both written against the Third Restatement's balancing model.

### 3. United States v. Bank of Nova Scotia, 691 F.2d 1384 (11th Cir. 1982)

Facts. A federal grand jury investigating tax and narcotics offences subpoenaed a Canadian bank's records held at its Nassau (Bahamas) and Antigua branches about one customer's accounts. The subpoena was served on the bank's Miami agency on 23 September 1981. The bank refused, citing Bahamian bank secrecy law, which made disclosure without customer consent or a Bahamian court order a crime. The evidence conflicted on how far Miami controlled Nassau's records. The bank's affidavit said a Bahamian court could order disclosure only if the conduct was criminal under Bahamian law, and not where it was solely a US tax offence. The government never showed the documents were relevant and necessary (footnote 3). The district court compelled production, the bank's Miami agent formally refused before the grand jury, and the bank was held in civil contempt.

Issues. The bank raised three arguments: insufficient grounds, due process, and comity. The packet reproduces only Part IV (comity), and only the last of the bank's distinctions of Field.

Reasoning. The court applied the Restatement (Second) § 40 balancing test, which footnote 7 reproduces. Its factors are vital national interests, hardship to the person, where the required conduct occurs, nationality, and likely effectiveness of enforcement. The court held the case controlled by In re Grand Jury Proceedings (Field) (5th Cir. 1976). There, contempt was upheld against a Cayman bank officer who refused to testify while present in the US.

The court rejected three further points:

- Records located abroad. Disclosure would happen in the US, and the affront to the Bahamas occurs wherever the records sit.
- Bahamian judicial assistance as an alternative. It was slow, costly and uncertain, and unavailable for pure US tax offences. The court also said it would make the US ask permission to do something lawful under its own law.
- The interest balance. The grand jury's role and revenue collection ("the financial integrity of the republic", quoting Field) outweighed Bahamian privacy. The Bahamian statute already allowed disclosure to many officials and to Bahamian courts, so it was "hardly a blanket guarantee of privacy" (citing Payner). The court called it incongruous for a US court to protect a customer more than a Bahamian court would.

Holding. Affirmed. Absent direction from Congress and the Executive, courts will not let foreign law thwart US criminal process. The court regretted the bank's position between two sovereigns but accepted it as a cost of international commerce.

My read (opinion). The balancing is lopsided by construction. US interests are described at the level of institutions (the grand jury, the revenue system), while Bahamian interests are reduced to one customer's privacy under a leaky statute. That asymmetry is exactly what the Harvard Note attacks. The jurisdictional hook is also striking. A single Miami agency office was enough to reach records in the Bahamas. Scale that hook up from one branch office to dollar clearing and correspondent banking and you get the secondary-sanctions machinery in the next reading.

4. Anna Sauerbrey, "The Failure of Europe's Feeble Muscle Flexing", New York Times, 10 February 2020

After Trump left the JCPOA in May 2018, the US reimposed and extended sanctions on Iran. These included secondary sanctions, which penalise foreign firms and individuals who trade with Iran, alongside the primary ban on US persons. Their force comes from US market size, the dollar, and US legal or practical control of payment systems. Analyst David Jalilvand's point is that almost every firm touches the US through its bank, insurer or reinsurer.

What Instex was. France, Germany and the UK created Instex in January 2019, after the EU announced it in September 2018. It was meant to be an EU body but became a private company, hosted in Paris and headed by a German. It works as a clearing house. Instex and an Iranian counterpart record the value of trade in each direction and pay exporters and importers on their own side, so no money crosses the line.

What happened. Staffing was hard because the US threatened to cut anyone involved out of the US financial system. A year on, the first "test" transaction was medical goods worth under €1 million. It was fully compliant with US sanctions, the firm and banks were kept secret, there were no Iranian exports in return, and Instex itself funded the payment.

The author's argument. Europe lacks the financial infrastructure for strategic autonomy. The Suleimani killing in January 2020 showed Washington would risk European security without consultation. China and Russia offered to join Instex, and Germany declined. She predicted a Trump re-election in 2020 might tip Europe towards decoupling.

Postscript (my knowledge, about 85% confident). Instex handled essentially that one transaction and its shareholders wound it up in 2023.

Why it is in a jurisdiction packet. It shows enforcement jurisdiction exercised through private intermediaries rather than courts, with the dollar system acting as the territorial nexus. The article omits the EU Blocking Statute (Regulation 2271/96, updated 2018), which forbids EU firms from complying with the US sanctions. That places European companies in the same double bind the Bank of Nova Scotia faced, at continental scale.

### 5. Note, "Constructing the State Extraterritorially", 103 Harv. L. Rev. 1273 (1990)

The opening puzzle. Noriega, a Panamanian, was arraigned in Miami in January 1990 for drug crimes committed abroad and faced 145 years. Meanwhile Boureslan, a US citizen, could not use Title VII against Aramco, a US company, for discrimination he suffered in Saudi Arabia. Territory and nationality cannot explain both results.

Part I, the national interest. The Restatement asks two questions: is there a territorial or national nexus, and is jurisdiction reasonable given competing interests? The Note argues every modern case turns on the content of the "national interest", and that this concept is endlessly malleable. It gives three pairs:

- Aramco. The majority invoked respect for Saudi sovereignty. Judge King, dissenting, invoked America's international image to reach the opposite result.
- Patel. The district court said excluding undocumented workers from the FLSA discouraged illegal immigration. The Eleventh Circuit said including them did, by removing employers' incentive to hire them.
- Filartiga and Tel-Oren. In Filartiga, human rights litigation served US interests and the torturer was hostis humani generis. In Tel-Oren, Judge Bork said adjudicating the claim would make the US an "officious interloper and an international busybody".

The Note proposes treating the national interest as an arena of contending particular claims made by groups inside and outside the US. Jurisdictional lines could then be drawn transnationally, according to normative views.

Part II.A, why the national frame persists. The jurist's mental map treats the state as a bounded nation with a fixed population and a discoverable collective will. The Note traces this to Sieyès and the French Revolution, and uses Deutsch on nationalism. On this view courts "refine the fit at the edges", and jurisdictional rhetoric runs on metaphors of inside and outside, belonging and banishment.

Part II.B, three effects of national rhetoric.

- It naturalises the nation.
- It eclipses transnational solidarities.
- It substitutes national categories for substantive reasoning, which hides normative choices.

Language both reflects and creates community. The Note draws on Cohen's symbolic community, Anderson's "imagined" nation and Bourdieu's "classification struggle". The footnotes on aliens (Eisentrager, Mezei, Jean v. Nelson, Korematsu) show how the inside/outside binary strips rights.

Part II.C, a new conception of the state. The state is redefined as a constellation of authoritative exercises of jurisdiction over people, events and property. Jurisdictional decisions define the state, and the state is no prior entity that decides whether to assert them. Membership becomes graded.

- Boureslan in the US is tightly bound to the state (drug laws, employment taxes, Title VII).
- Boureslan in Saudi Arabia is looser (drug laws and taxes, but no Title VII).
- A worker on a Saudi-flagged ship entering New York is looser still, but still within US drug law.

The state reaches "a foreigner who tortures but not an American who discriminates". Outcomes need not change, but justification must. A judge deciding whether child labour law follows US firms abroad would have to defend a normative choice on its merits.

Part III, the alternative discourse. Transnational alliances replace borders as the map.

The imperialism objection. Critics may say this is US imperialism in transnational dress. The Note gives two replies:

- Nations remain one important pull among several, sometimes decisive, so the aim is to denaturalise national discourse while keeping it.
- Normative orders cut across borders. Every jurisdictional decision, whether asserting or declining jurisdiction, imposes some transnational group's norms on another.

My assessment (opinion, moderate confidence). The diagnosis of malleability is persuasive and Bank of Nova Scotia illustrates it well. The remedy is weaker. "Decide on the merits" moves judicial discretion without disciplining it. The second reply to the imperialism objection also ignores power. Which transnational coalition wins usually tracks who controls the courts and the currency, as the Instex reading shows.

### 6. Paul Schiff Berman, "The Globalization of Jurisdiction", 151 U. Pa. L. Rev. 311 (2002)

Writing after a decade of "cyberspace" and "globalisation" talk, Berman argues that jurisdiction is the main site where societies debate community definition, sovereignty and legitimacy. He says current frameworks wrongly assume territorially bounded nation-states are the only relevant jurisdictional units, and ignore the anthropology, geography and sociology of how people actually experience belonging.

Cosmopolitan pluralism. He calls his proposal a "cosmopolitan pluralist" conception of jurisdiction. "Cosmopolitan" here means multiple attachments, local through global. It does not mean universalism or one world community. Following Doreen Massey, communities become "articulated moments in networks of social relations". Courts would ask what community affiliations a dispute actually engages, rather than counting territorial contacts, effects or interests. This could restrain some extraterritorial assertions and justify others not currently recognised. He places it between strict territorialism and full universal jurisdiction.

The pluralist move. Drawing on legal pluralism scholarship (Merry, Engel, Griffiths, Galanter), Berman says non-state communities also assert jurisdiction and generate norms. He separates asserting jurisdiction from enforcing a judgment. An assertion without coercive power still opens space for articulating a norm.

His example is Pinochet. Judge Garzón's 1998 Spanish arrest warrant led to the House of Lords ruling that Pinochet had no head-of-state immunity for torture after 1988. Jack Straw then released him on health grounds in March 2000. The prosecution failed, but it changed the environment for human rights prosecutions. Enforcement therefore depends on persuasion, and recognition of judgments becomes the terrain on which rival conceptions of community compete for legitimacy.

Two normative components.

- For state courts, attend to the social meaning of community and space. Berman claims judges already do this intuitively in hard cases.
- Recognise and evaluate non-state jurisdictional assertions, from international tribunals to NGO quasi-courts to private standard-setters, including transnational "common law" built up through practice.

Relation to the Note. Berman extends the Note's constructivism in two directions. He gives jurisdiction to non-state actors, and he makes the assertion/enforcement gap central.

### Threads across the packet

The Restatement offers balancing. Nova Scotia shows the forum state doing the balancing and winning. Instex shows that balancing hardly matters when enforcement runs through dollar plumbing. The Note says the national interest being balanced is a fiction. Berman says the useful question is which communities a claim engages and whether the assertion can persuade.

Here is a provocation worth testing in section (my view, speculative). On Berman's own terms, the US Treasury's sanctions reach is the most successful "non-territorial jurisdiction" in the world. Instex failed because Europe tried to answer a network-based jurisdiction with a legal entity.
