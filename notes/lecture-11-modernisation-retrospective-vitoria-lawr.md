### Course Overview and Running Puzzles

- Roughly halfway through the course; today begins a retrospective phase
- Four recurring puzzles framing the arc:
- How law became more ubiquitous as it became more uncertain and plural
- The persistence of two voices: traditional/sovereignty-based vs. modern/sociological
- What it means to call something “legal” or “illegal” in advocacy contexts
- Whether there is a coherent international legal system or order at all

### Two Maps of the Post-War Order

- Surface map: relatively equal states cooperating through institutions
- Jurisdictional map: deeply unequal, shaped by willingness and capacity to assert jurisdiction
- State = sum of jurisdictions actually exercised, not powers nominally held
- Tension between these two maps is a defining 21st-century puzzle
- Was the post-war order a stable order among equals, or a hierarchy of differential powers?
- Benevolent hegemon narrative vs. post-colonial hierarchy narrative

### Modernization Story: Key Innovations

- Relativization of norms: persuasiveness varies by situation; boundary between legal and non-legal softens
- Fragmentation: diverse norms with variable effects; statehood itself fragmented (e.g., Palestine vs. Belgium)
- New modes of legal reasoning: claims weighed, not on/off; factor balancing; title as progressive acquisition
- Differential power mapped jurisdictionally, not territorially
- State responsibility and deterrence logic converge
- Countermeasures/proportionality in state responsibility mirrors cyber-deterrence strategy
- Both involve calibrated pressure to enforce norms without a central enforcer

### Victoria and the “Primitive” Scholars (Koskenniemi Article)

- Article written in 1986 as a critique of the modernist moment’s self-congratulatory history
- “Primitive” label applied to pre-Westphalian scholars (Victoria, Grotius, etc.) to defamiliarize them
- Key argument: contemporary scholarship wrongly casts Victoria as a forerunner of modern international law
- Victoria’s framework was on/off: you either had the right or you didn’t
- Modern reading projects proceduralism and gradualism onto him retroactively
- Citation practice of the period: wild, eclectic, no visible methodology
- Bible, Caesar, Aquinas used interchangeably; no meditation on when to cite whom
- Humanists expanded the corpus further; still no principled selection
- By contrast, late 19th-century scholars became obsessed with normative pedigree
- Naturalism vs. positivism, opinio juris, state practice: a gigantic methodological machine
- Diversity in the primitive period: diverse authorities, but belief in one universal moral order
- Contrast with modern diversity: plural legal orders, no assumed unity

### Riles on Lawrence and Colonial International Law

- Lawrence: late 19th-century British international law scholar at the height of colonial expansion
- Riles’s argument: international law simultaneously played two registers
- Communication/aspiration: European states coordinating as equals in a shared civilizational project
- Control/repression: law as a tool to manage and subordinate the colonial periphery
- The state as the linchpin: the triangle form unified both projects under one legal concept
- Paradox: statehood had more reality in Latin America than in much of Europe at the time
- Key quote from Riles: “Communication is understandable in the language of control as well as aspiration, while colonialism evokes both a utopian hope and a repressive image”
- Both projects contained seeds of the other; the unity was constitutive, not accidental
- Culture placed at the center: Riles (trained as an anthropologist) reads Lawrence for what law does culturally
- European cultural unity as aspiration; colonial otherness as the defining outside

### Anghie and TWAIL

- Anghie’s project: show how the international legal order reproduced hierarchy during decolonization
- Target: the prior generation of Third World international lawyers (e.g., ICJ judges) who embraced the liberal order
- Argument: they were “in cahoots” with 19th-century European thinking without realizing it
- Sovereignty for the Third World meant something different: not fullness of power, but its absence
- “Inclusion by assimilation”: sovereignty granted on European terms, disconnected from local identity
- Sovereignty in the non-European world = alienation and subordination, not empowerment
- Standard of civilization critique: human rights potentially continuous with the civilizing mission
- To be sovereign, you had to meet European standards of how to treat European nationals
- Human rights framework may reproduce that conditionality
- Haiti example: achieved sovereignty via revolution, then spent over a century paying reparations to France with no compensation for enslaved people

### Becker’s Counter-Argument and the NIEO

- Becker’s critique of Anghie: no room for Third World resistance, strategy, or agency
- Latin Americans at Montevideo actively pushed non-intervention as a global legal norm
- Weak-hand strategies matter; resistance was real even if incomplete
- New International Economic Order (1970s): major attempt to restructure global economic relations through the UN
- Driven by post-oil-shock enthusiasm and newly independent states
- Collapsed under debt crisis, US interference, and the ideological shift to neoliberalism
- Core demand: national control over natural resources (nationalization)
- Anghie’s view: postcolonial leaders misread their own sovereignty; real power lay with first-world banks
- Becker’s current projects:
- The Incas as a pre-colonial empire with their own legal advocacy (archives in Seville)
- Small-state strategies for using sovereignty in the late 20th century
