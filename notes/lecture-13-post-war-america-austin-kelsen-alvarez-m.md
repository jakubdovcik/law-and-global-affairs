## my notes

### Context and Framing

- Post-WWII US found itself in a vastly expanded global position, building on interwar legal renewal ideas
- Key backdrop: Allied wartime collaboration, Nazi/Japanese imperial counterexample, and the sudden emergence of the Soviet Union as an adversarial power
- The “authoritarianism” concept invented in the 1940s-50s: linking Soviet and Nazi systems as equivalents
- Thinkers covered span the arc of American postwar hegemony through its unraveling (Iraq War ~2003 as the inflection point)
- Political valences of the figures:
- Republicans: McDougal, Wedgwood
- Democrats: Slaughter, Schachter, Jessup
- Centrist: Henkin (Columbia; human rights and UN)

### The Austin Problem

- Austin: law “properly so-called” is a command from a determinate sovereign, backed by sanction
- International law = “law improperly so-called,” set by general opinion, not a sovereign
- This became the challenge every postwar casebook had to answer
- Student observation: Austin slips between “positive law” and “law” generally
- Suggests there’s something law-like without a sovereign, still tied to sanction
- Austin may not have felt the need to clarify; the strict identification came later
- Austin’s own text hints at the answer: moral sanctions, fear of provoking general hostility
- The “resurrection” of Austin as a critical problem reflects the hardening of positivism
- Before that hardening (e.g., Marshall-era common law), the problem seemed less acute
- Austin becomes a useful “easy target”: once students think “I guess they don’t have that,” the sociological and functional answers flow naturally

### Kelsen: Law as a Matter of Regard

- Kelsen arrives in the US via lecture series (likely Yale), partly seeking a job, explaining international law to an American audience
- Core move: law is not discovered, it is a choice of how to regard the situation
- “It’s possible, though not necessary, to regard international law as true law”
- A plea to adopt the legal vocabulary as the better way to understand global affairs
- State = legal order (identity thesis), originally developed to answer the German constitutional question of why states can be bound
- The state is not a body that decides to exercise jurisdiction; it is the sum of its effective powers
- Analogy: just as God regarded scientifically is nature itself, the state as a person is only the anthropomorphic expression of its unity
- Key concept: efficacy
- Law is effective when the social order behind it is efficacious
- Power = efficacy of an order regulating mutual conduct; need not be a legal order
- Narrowing criterion: law is present when an act of force is regarded as either a delict or a sanction
- Decentralized enforcement: states discipline each other horizontally
- This makes international law “primitive,” analogous to anthropologists’ accounts of pre-state societies
- Direction of travel: the UN as a future superstate, just as primitive tribes evolved into states
- Student exchange on the state/law reversal:
- Not the state that backs law; law backs the state
- The state exists because people buy into the legal order behind it
- Ambiguity noted: Kelsen distinguishes legal order from morality/religion by sanction, but moral and religious orders can also be more effective than legal ones
- Student challenge: if sanction is the criterion, why isn’t excommunication law?
- Kelsen’s answer: decentralized coercive order, not restricted to the state alone

### Alvarez: Social Interdependence and a New Sovereignty

- Writing 25 years after 1922, now a judge, still optimistic after the second cataclysm
- Core idea: sovereignty is not the source of obligation (contra Lotus); it is an interactive social responsibility
- Obligations flow from social interdependence, not sovereign will
- Doctrine of rebus sic stantibus = principle of social justice (law changes with social conditions)
- Law is the result of social life; politics and law are closely related, not opposed
- Sovereignty redefined: “an international social function of a psychological character”
- The state is something that occurs within an international environment, not prior to it
- Contrast with Kelsen: Alvarez reads the “is” of social life and translates it into an “ought”
- Kelsen finds law in what becomes effective through sanction; Alvarez reads it from social interdependence
- Appeal to UN world: the UN as the highest institutionalized expression of the social order, not merely a clerk of great powers

### McDougal: World Power Process and Law as Policy

- Writing in the 1950s Cold War context; a right-wing cold warrior, but with a sophisticated theory of law and power
- Critique of the binary: the mistake is thinking it’s either “all political” or “all legal”
- Kennan’s view (law as “unobtrusive, almost feminine civilizer of national interest”) dismissed as too weak
- But the civilizing function of legal vocabulary was actually a durable and important idea since the late 19th century
- Law is not a “frozen cake of doctrine”; it is the outcome of a continuous, interactive decision-making process
- Power is not just military force; it is entangled with institutions, authority, legitimacy, and ethics
- Anticipates the hard power/soft power distinction
- “World power process”: continuous formulation and reformulation of policies through interaction across institutional spaces
- Disaggregated actors (not just state departments), including private society voices
- Practices of tolerance and intolerance emerge through the process, not single events
- McDougal’s Cold War ethics: international law should not be agnostic between the two sides
- Legality as a carrier of the legitimacy of American action; law as a power ally
- Practical example: argued for legality of hydrogen bomb testing as necessary for the Free World to prevail
- Student challenge: is this really different from political realism in practice?
- McDougal’s answer: it’s a principled struggle over values and the future of humanity, not just interest maximization
- The world power process is not about everyone getting along; it is a serious, generation-long contest

### Schachter on Hammarskjöld: Law as Diplomatic Toolkit

- Schachter: Columbia, Democrat, refugee, antagonist of McDougal’s Republicans; writing in 1961 as the UN seemed to be failing
- Hammarskjöld as the embodiment of inspired legal diplomacy
- Five principles of Hammarskjöld’s approach to law:
- No sharp distinction between law and policy
- Think in principles, not rules (avoid fettering yourself with doctrine)
- Work with flexibility and adroitness, ad hoc rather than by engineering blueprint
- Exploit the contradictory nature of legal principles as a feature, not a defect
- Weigh and balance opposing tendencies (cites Paul Freund on constitutional balancing)
- Indeterminacy as a feature:
- The more open-ended the legal framework, the more actors can enter the conversation
- If law clearly determined winners and losers, losers would simply deny its existence
- Ambiguity entices all parties into the legal vocabulary
- Practical application: in Third World decolonization conflicts, Hammarskjöld could offer principled compromise
- Each side goes home claiming the outcome vindicated their principles
- Law as compromise-finding, not just advocacy
- Jus cogens: people tend to invoke it for things they feel very strongly about
- A sophisticated diplomat treats jus cogens claims strategically, not as objective truth
- Must read why an actor believes something is jus cogens and work with that in the power process

### Law vs. Politics: The Recurring Tension

- The “it’s all politics” objection: a persistent strand, especially among international relations scholars
- Game theory and deterrence strategy (Harvard/MIT) offered public-choice explanations for state behavior that bypassed law entirely
- Kissinger: mutually assured destruction is not credible; need smaller, flexible nuclear options
- Sociological turn vs. formal positivism: the period saw the defeat of the purely philosophical/formal image of law
- Replaced by functional equivalence: if it looks like law, works like law, we call it law
- Sociology and anthropology of law expanding the concept simultaneously (informal contracts, customary land title, family arrangements)
- The legitimacy loop: law only legitimates if people believe legality is a meaningful criterion
- Those believers must have some significance in the overall story
- Henkin (1980): a sermon of belief, insisting the cup is half full, written at the moment of major international shifts
- Auto-interpretation (Leo Gross): the doctrinal transformation of interstate struggle into legal struggle
- States don’t just say “I want it”; they say “you violated my entitlement”
- That transformation is a civilizing move; the postwar order was a work of cultural and social imagination, not just institutional design
