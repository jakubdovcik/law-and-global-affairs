## what Kennedy stressed

- **The arc:** from norms to process. Normativity is relativised, deducible from sovereignty, interaction, law itself, humanity and community. Adjudication ([[lotus]], [[barcelona-traction]], [[nottebohm]]) was de facto rule-making, and by the 1960s–70s it was criticised as a weak legislative technique.
- **After 1989:** NGOs, foundations (Ford, Hewlett), new courts, and **norm entrepreneurs** (Kofi Annan, Greenpeace) who 'claim norms into existence' through advocacy and shaming. Then 'the bloom came off the rose' (fragmentation).
- **The paradox:** 'the more plastic the vocabulary, the more it is used'. Ambiguity helps both advocacy and diplomatic middle ground, which is why deduction from principles survived legal realism.
- **ICL's birth problems:**
  - Amnesty flipped from demanding amnesty to demanding prosecution;
  - it is criminal law without a sovereign;
  - the ICC was first proposed for the drug trade.
- **Personifying atrocity** (Milošević, Putin) 'lets the broader machinery off the hook'. Show trials put the process itself on trial; Vergès in the Barbie case was 'smart lawyering' ([[koskenniemi-impunity]]).
- **The Putin warrant, weighed:**
  - for: restricted movement, a signal, victims, Eastern European memory;
  - against: harder negotiations, a bargaining chip, raised expectations;
  - alternatives: trials in absentia, truth commissions, people's tribunals, Stolpersteine.
- [[bolton-icc-2018]]: once again, hegemons and very small states resist, and **middle powers are the ICC's constituency**.
- **Genocide's social and legal meanings diverge** ([[drumbl-smouldering]]). A narrow Gaza ruling risks the Court's legitimacy. [[engle-grip]]: ICL narrowed feminist goals. **'Legalizing a norm sharpens it but also constrains it'**, and what falls outside the frame can matter as much as what falls inside. The ICC's Africa problem.

### 20th-Century International Law: The Arc of the Lecture

- Movement from norms to process across the century
- Normativity relativized: rules deducible from sovereignty, interaction, law itself, humanity, and community
- Adjudication (Lotus, Barcelona Traction, Nottebohm) as de facto rulemaking
- Criticized by the '60s–'70s as weak legislative technique and not persuasive
- Pressure shifted toward treaties, bilateral arrangements, political branches

### Post-1989 Expansion of International Law

- End of Cold War opened space for substantive norm-building
- New actors: NGOs, foundations (Ford, Hewlett), international organizations
- Proliferation of dispute resolution mechanisms (Law of the Sea, 1981 onward)
- New courts and tribunals, including the ICC
- Norm entrepreneurship: actors like Greenpeace claim norms into existence through advocacy and shaming
- Ecocide, genocide, human dignity floated as emerging norms
- Goal shifted from rules to broader values: human dignity as an underlying principle
- Bloom off the rose quickly: fragmentation, lack of unity across courts by early 21st century

### Why Deduction from Principles Persists

- Legal realists moved away from deduction, yet international law kept using it
- Key reason: it seems to work as advocacy
- Parties hurl whatever arguments might persuade an audience
- Norm entrepreneurs repeat claims until they stick; consumer boycotts, UN declarations follow
- Paradox: the more plastic the vocabulary, the more it is used
- Ambiguity makes it useful both for advocacy and for finding diplomatic middle ground

### International Criminal Law: Origins and Tensions

- Anti-impunity norm emerged from post-1989 enthusiasm
- Amnesty International shifted from advocating amnesty to demanding prosecution
- Conceptual problems from the start:
- Criminal law without a sovereign to define crimes
- Disaggregated, self-appointed prosecution across national courts
- Diverse excuses, priorities, and standards across jurisdictions
- Original ICC proposal centered on drug trade, not war crimes; redirected to crimes of international significance
- Solution: a unified treaty court (Rome Statute) held to high evidentiary and human rights standards

### Show Trials and the Milosevic Problem

- International criminal trials carry symbolic weight beyond individual guilt
- Truth production, deterrence, norm reinforcement, survivor recovery all cited as goals
- Tension: individualizing guilt vs. structural or collective responsibility
- Personifying atrocity in one leader (Milosevic, Putin) lets the broader machinery off the hook
- “Show trial” critique: the trial’s constructed nature itself goes on trial
- Defendants can use the platform to attack the legitimacy of the process
- Barbie case: defense attacked French colonialism, diverting attention from Barbie’s own crimes
- Seen as smart lawyering but undermined the prosecution’s narrative

### Ukraine, Putin, and the ICC Arrest Warrant

- Western international lawyers pushed for prosecution of Putin as norm reinforcement
- Arguments for pursuing it:
- Restricts Putin’s movement and diplomatic options
- Signals illegitimacy of the war; satisfies victim communities
- Eastern European historical memory gives trials particular weight
- Arguments against:
- Complicates negotiation: hard to sit across from an indicted war criminal
- Arrest warrant becomes a bargaining chip to be lifted in any settlement
- Promise of justice unlikely to be fulfilled raises expectations that may harm victims
- Alternative mechanisms raised: trials in absentia, truth commissions, people’s tribunals, memorial plaques (Stolpersteine model)

### Bolton, Sovereignty, and the U.S. Rejection of the ICC

- Bolton’s argument: democratic sovereignty means citizens should only be tried under their own laws
- ICC imposes norms not democratically chosen; jurisdiction is arbitrary
- Counterpoints raised in discussion:
- U.S. has not prosecuted its own soldiers for torture in Iraq
- Operating in other states means being subject to their norms
- Same sovereignty argument made by Myanmar, authoritarian states
- U.S. response: bilateral non-cooperation treaties, sanctions on ICC members
- Not just non-participation but active effort to undermine the court
- Observation: hegemonic powers and very small states both resist; middle powers are the ICC’s natural constituency

### Genocide, Legal Narrowing, and the Limits of Legalization

- Genocide has both a social meaning and a legal meaning; they diverge
- Legal definition is narrow: requires specific intent, precise criteria
- Social meaning overflows the legal definition; conflict between the two is inevitable
- ICJ Gaza case as live example:
- Non-lawyers see a fundamental moral wrong; legal finding may not match that intuition
- A narrow ruling risks delegitimizing the court in the eyes of those who brought the case
- Feminist critique (Nagle): international criminal law narrowed feminist goals
- Focused on rape as the paradigmatic crime; fixed women as violated victims
- Structural issues and broader range of harms crowded out
- Broader takeaway: legalizing a norm sharpens it but also constrains it
- What gets left outside the legal frame can be as significant as what gets in
- ICC’s Africa problem: prosecutions concentrated on African leaders, reinforcing perception of neocolonial project

## my reading prep — topic 6 readings: detailed summary

### 1. Martti Koskenniemi, "Between Impunity and Show Trials" (Max Planck UNYB 6, 2002)

Framing. Milošević opened his defence at The Hague on 12 February 2002 by accusing NATO and the West of complicity in destroying Yugoslavia and of aggression in 1999. Koskenniemi uses this to pose his central problem. International criminal law oscillates between two poles. One is the wish to punish individuals responsible for mass atrocity. The other is the danger that the trial becomes a show trial. Individual criminal responsibility is a poor instrument for grasping large political crises, and a criminal trial struggles to state the "truth" of complex events involving great powers, the EU and the UN.

I. Why punish? The liberal rule-of-law view holds that nobody stands outside the law, so leaders must answer for their crimes. Koskenniemi then undermines each standard justification.

Retribution is inadequate. Arendt said hanging Göring was necessary but "totally inadequate", because such crimes shatter the legal order. Jaspers said of Eichmann that something other than law was at stake. Whether Milošević goes to prison cannot answer 200,000 deaths.

Truth and didactics are fragile. The Eichmann trial was justified as a way to publicise the Nazi war against the Jews and to anchor Israeli national history. Eichmann himself was a "cog"; Wiesel suggested freeing him and Arendt suggested handing him to the UN. Recording truth is also defended as therapy for victims and a condition of social reconciliation. The evidence of didactic effect is weak. In Germany, 78% thought Nuremberg just at the time and only 38% four years later. By 1952 only 10% approved of the follow-up trials, seen widely as victor's justice. German domestic prosecutions peaked at 39 convictions a year and fell below ten by 1976. After South Africa's TRC, only 17% saw a positive effect and two-thirds thought race relations had worsened. Lawrence Douglas warns that law's adversarial formalism treats all accounts as prima facie equal and may legitimise negationism.

Arendt's purism. For Arendt a trial's only purpose is justice; any didactic aim corrupts it, which is why she called the Eichmann trial a show trial staged by Ben-Gurion.

Deterrence is implausible. The Rome Statute drafters leaned heavily on deterrence. Koskenniemi doubts it. "Radical evil" is non-instrumental and calculates nothing. Much 20th-century atrocity came from people trying to do good (Besançon on communism; Himmler's speeches). Arendt's "banality of evil" means perpetrators act as conformists inside a criminal normality, and criminal law cannot teach heroism.

Symbolism. Borneman's view that a trial ritually affirms a community's moral principles is the most persuasive. Transitional trials have been few, selective and moderate, with widespread amnesties; mass criminality forces selectivity (over 100,000 suspects in the British and American zones in 1946; about 120,000 detainees in Rwanda in 2001, hence gacaca). Victims often want recognition more than punishment.

Key conclusion. Nationally, a "show trial" can be acceptable when it affirms a real moral community and sits alongside compensation, lustration, truth commissions and archives. Internationally, no such community exists beyond the "self-congratulatory" idea of an "international community". Every failure to prosecute becomes a scandal and every judgement looks too small. With deterrence discredited, truth-telling becomes the Milošević trial's main justification, which leads to the next section.

II. Of truth and context. Legal truth and historical truth differ. The wider the context needed to understand individual guilt, the clearer the limits of criminal procedure; truth commissions can canvass far more. In ordinary domestic crime the context is uncontested. In transitions, the context itself is disputed: how to judge "criminal normality" in an Unrechtsstaat, passivity, or the failure to be a hero. Internationally it gets worse. Milošević's "Greater Serbia" narrative collides with secessionist self-determination narratives, and the Western account relies on a view of atavistic Balkan ethnic hatred that downplays political interests.

Morgenthau (1929) argued that legal disputes capture only a narrow slice of a political "tension" and can distort the whole. Individualisation abstracts from that context; Del Ponte insisted the Serbian nation was not on trial, only an individual. Koskenniemi draws on the intentionalist/functionalist debate among Holocaust historians. Individualisation is politically loaded because it leaves structures intact and can serve as an alibi for whole populations ("Hitlerism", "Stalinism"). German prosecutions after 1945 illustrate this. Once lesser charges were time-barred, only murder remained, and a definition requiring individual murderous intent (Mordlust) let most Nazis escape and reintegrate into the Bonn republic.

He refuses to rank the individual or structural lens a priori. Sometimes the individual focus fits; for Nazi crimes or Stasi collaboration the context is the better frame. A trial built around individual guilt therefore guarantees no historical lesson and may obstruct one by exonerating structures.

Individualisation is also impossible in practice. Defences invoke context (Krajina, the KLA in Kosovo, chains of command at Omarska and Srebrenica, the Račak inquiry). Since Stasi agents or apartheid hit squades were conformists within a political normality, Milošević can claim the trial is really a trial of the Serbian nation. Here Koskenniemi applies Lyotard's différend: a conflict regulated in one party's idiom, so the other's wrong cannot be expressed. His example: German bombing of Coventry (about 1,200 dead) counts as a war crime while Allied area bombing (perhaps 600,000 dead) does not, which is only intelligible if one already accepts the Allied framing. Putting Milošević in the dock, and not Western leaders, presupposes the Western view of context. From his standpoint the trial can only be a show trial, and participating means accepting defeat.

IV. The politics of truth. To reach truth, the accused must speak, which risks a propaganda stage. This worried Nuremberg (Göring gloated at outmanoeuvring Jackson) and explains the Guantánamo military commissions. Milošević plays to his domestic audience.

Jacques Vergès, on Milošević's advisory list, theorised the "trial of rupture": the defence attacks the prosecuting system itself, as opposed to the "trial of connivance", which disputes facts within shared terms. Precedents include Socrates, Louis XVI, Prometheus, Zola's J'accuse and Dimitrov at the Reichstag fire trial, who refused an alibi and used the dock for the proletarian cause.

The Barbie trial (Lyon, 1987) is the exemplar. Barbie ran the Lyon Gestapo's information section, deported the 44 Jewish children of Izieu, and tortured resistance members including Jean Moulin. He escaped via US counter-intelligence to Bolivia. Two politics of history followed.

First, the Cour de Cassation widened "crimes against humanity" to include acts against the resistance, flattering a Fifth Republic image of France as a nation of fighters. Frossard objected that this erased the specificity of the Shoah, killing someone "under the pretext of having been born", and Koskenniemi agrees, also attacking Wilhelm Grewe's history of international law for omitting the Shoah.

Second, Vergès ran a tu quoque defence with Algerian and Congolese co-counsel. He never tried to exculpate Barbie and instead equated his acts with French colonial torture in Algeria and Indochina, citing Sétif on 8 May 1945. His line, roughly, was that France bows before Izieu because it remembers Algeria's children. He denied nothing about the Holocaust and relativised it by reframing its context.

### 2. John Bolton, remarks on the ICC (Federalist Society, 10 September 2018)

Bolton announces US policy: no cooperation, assistance or membership; "let the ICC die on its own", as it is "already dead to us". He recounts the 2002 "unsigning" of the Rome Statute, the American Servicemembers' Protection Act ("Hague Invasion Act") authorising force to free detained personnel, and roughly 100 bilateral non-surrender agreements he negotiated. The trigger was the Prosecutor's November 2017 request to investigate US personnel for detainee abuse in Afghanistan.

He gives five objections. Sovereignty and consent: the Court claims jurisdiction over non-party nationals, and it fuses executive and judicial power because the Prosecutor is an organ of the Court; oversight by the Assembly of States Parties (Venezuela, DRC, the Palestinian Authority) is inadequate, and he cites misconduct allegations against the first Prosecutor. Vague crimes: aggression could reach the bin Laden raid, Syria strikes or Israeli self-defence; he notes a proposed "ecocide" crime and invokes Allied bombing of Germany and Japan. Failure: over $1.5 billion spent for eight convictions; "hard men of history" are deterred only by American "righteous might". The Court is dangerous for both its potential strength and its manifest weakness. Redundancy: US courts suffice, and complementarity is "farcical" given the Afghanistan request. Rejection: 70+ non-member states covering two-thirds of humanity, African withdrawals over neo-colonial targeting, and threatened investigations of Israel. He also announces closure of the PLO office in Washington.

Threatened measures: more non-surrender agreements; entry bans, financial sanctions and US prosecution of ICC judges and prosecutors and of companies or states that assist; linking aid and intelligence-sharing to non-cooperation; Security Council action to restrict jurisdiction.

3. Krever et al., "On international law and Gaza" (London Review of International Law 12(2), 2024), excerpts

Unattributed contribution (opening fragment; author not visible in the excerpt). How institutions respond to Gaza, framed as genocide, will define them for decades. The binaries that sustain international law (legal/illegal, theory/practice, critical/doctrinal) are losing meaning. The US has effectively exited international legal argument: it once offered legal cover (Kosovo "illegal but legitimate", Iraq, the torture memos, Libya as R2P) and now barely argues, widening the gap between the "rules-based order" and international law (Dugard). Legal concepts such as genocide and starvation serve an existential struggle for meaning. Citing Darryl Li on a "unique moment of Palestinian liberation", the author asks whether international law could be used militantly, for coalition-building and self-determination, instead of for compromise.

Zinaida Miller, "Times of Violence, Times of Justice". Temporality is an instrument of power. Calling 7 October "sudden" decontextualised it and erased 17 years of siege, without implying anyone deserved their fate. In the battle over "long violence", Israel, backed by the US and Germany, framed the attack as the deadliest day for Jews since the Holocaust, casting it as timeless antisemitism; Palestinians foregrounded the ongoing Nakba. "Slow violence" (Nixon) and settler colonialism as structure (Wolfe) capture everyday dispossession. Using Foucault, she maps law onto three temporalities. Punishment looks to a discrete past: the ICC, with Khan's warrant applications, can judge individual acts and cannot capture long violence; South Africa's ICJ case wove in settler colonialism and apartheid, though with limits. Discipline creates an infinite present: occupation is lawful only if temporary, Oslo disguised permanence, apartheid has no built-in end, and checkpoints "steal time". Security governs a risky future: Gaza's future is being erased (scholasticide, domicide, pregnant women and children) while Palestinians as a population are constructed as a threat. Oslo declared its own Year Zero and ratified existing hierarchies. The past matters because it maps today's distribution of power and resources, and the real remedy lies in redistributing them, beyond anything courts deliver.

Mark Drumbl, "Smouldering Semantics". Lemkin's original concept (1944) was broad: destruction of a group's political, social, cultural, linguistic and economic foundations. State interests narrowed it in the 1948 Convention, and the ICTY, ICTR, Rome Statute and ICJ reproduced it, fossilising it into custom. Public discourse on Gaza uses "genocide" far more broadly. If the ICJ finds no genocide, believers will not recant; they will call the Court wrong or stodgy, or dismiss the legal definition, and a finding of genocide would provoke parallel dismissals elsewhere. His counterfactual: had Lemkin left the word to national social discourse for a generation, condemnation might have flowed through political and economic channels where precision matters less. Courts have become primus inter pares of condemnation. Law privileges incarceration and state remedies and reduces blame to one defendant, obscuring the abandonment by many actors (other states, Hamas) that he calls a sine qua non of genocide. He welcomes campus divestment demands for opening wider causal conversations.

Unattributed fragment ("let it burn"). Legal engagement risks losing and legitimising oppressive structures; unless international law serves an anti-imperialist strategy against the material structures of the ongoing Nakba, the author seconds a colleague: "let it burn".

Tor Krever, "Scurrying to The Hague". Borrowing Qabbani's 1995 poem on those who rushed to normalisation after Oslo, Krever targets legal "scurriers" who treat international law as inherently progressive. Framing Gaza as illegality naturalises the structures producing violence (Brecht: nobody calls the riverbed violent). Critical scholars who once stressed indeterminacy now sign open letters and demand ICC warrants, showing faith at odds with the Court's record. The 2003 Iraq anti-war legalism is the warning precedent. The anti-imperial tradition sought revolutionary change; he questions casting the ANC's lawyers as an anti-imperial vanguard and warns against laundering domestic reaction. Birzeit students see no relevance in the ICJ proceedings. International law remains an "edgeless sword".

Sophie Rigney, "Justice Without Criminal Law" (opening only). She imagines a free Palestine (safe children, fed families, poets living into old age) and asks whether international criminal law can bring it about. The excerpt ends before her answer.

### 4. Karen Engle, The Grip of Sexual Violence in Conflict (2020), Introduction pp. 1–7, 12–15

The author is not named in the extract; I am fairly confident (about 90%) this is Engle's Stanford book.

Thesis. Since the rapes in the former Yugoslavia in the early 1990s, sexual violence in conflict has come to dominate feminist engagement with international law, and it occupies too large a place. It displaced three earlier commitments: the anti-militarism of the women's peace movement, sex-positive positions from the sex-work and pornography debates, and Third World feminist critiques of maldistribution, imperialism and cultural essentialism. It also elevated "structural-bias feminism", largely from the global North, which treats male sexual domination as the main obstacle to women's emancipation.

The "common sense". Five linked propositions: rape is the worst wartime crime; its harm lies largely in shame; perpetrators are individual male monsters; victims are innocent and mostly female; and prosecution will end sexual violence and promote peace. This fused with the human rights movement's turn to criminal law, which individualises responsibility, ignores bystanders and beneficiaries, and condemns conduct within wars instead of wars themselves.

Illustration. The 2014 UK Foreign Office video for the Hague–Jolie Global Summit (with the Preventing Sexual Violence Initiative, the April 2013 G8 Declaration, which Hague called addressing "the slave trade of our generation", a Security Council resolution and a Declaration of Commitment signed by 156 states). A child-drawn family idyll is invaded by soldiers; the mother is raped; perpetrators turn into growling monsters; a judge sends one to prison, bars multiply, and the family is restored with only the dog bandaged. The war apparently continues, but the video lets viewers forget it.

Section C, criminal law will end it. The video leaves no room for the banality of evil. In the mid-1980s feminists focused on domestic violence against women, used "naming and shaming", and built shelters and consciousness-raising; armed conflict and international criminal law were marginal. Criminal law became the preferred tool in the early 1990s through "carceral feminism" (Bernstein), the human rights turn to criminal law, and "governance feminism" (Halley), as feminists entered institutions that deferred to them. Deterrence claims (Hague, Bangura) come with almost no evidence. They are dangerous because they promise the impossible and divert resources from social, political and economic interventions that address structural causes.

### How the readings connect

The shared axis is individual versus structure. Koskenniemi (context as différend), Miller (criminal law's discrete past), Drumbl (reductionism to one defendant) and Engle (the "monster" narrative) all argue that criminalisation individualises what is systemic and thereby shields structures and bystanders.

The most provocative pairing is Bolton with the critical left. Bolton, Koskenniemi, Engle and Krever all doubt deterrence and all see international criminal justice as selective and political. Bolton even deploys Koskenniemi's Allied-bombing example. The disagreement is over the remedy: Bolton wants sovereign impunity backed by American force, while the critics want redistribution, political struggle or a broader vocabulary of condemnation. A strong seminar question (my suggestion) would be whether a Koskenniemi-style critique of ICL can avoid handing arguments to Bolton, or whether his closing thought, that participation in a différend means defeat, applies to the critics themselves.

Two small factual flags. Koskenniemi dates the Reichstag fire to 1934 (it was 1933, trial the same year), and the Sétif death toll he gives (15,000) is one estimate within a heavily disputed range.
