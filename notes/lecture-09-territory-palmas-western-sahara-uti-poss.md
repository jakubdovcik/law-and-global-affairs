### Palmas Arbitration (1928)

- US claim: successor to Spain, founded on discovery
- Dutch claim: continuous, effective exercise of sovereignty since 1677 treaty with local princes
- Arbitrator Max Huber decided for the Dutch on Grotian grounds: anti-discovery, effective exercise wins over paper title
- Key modernizing elements:
- Both parties had something; weighing, not on/off
- Progressive intensification: sovereignty must be displayed, but scale varies for remote/uninhabited islands
- Sovereignty as factual control, not just legal title
- Natives not counted in the analysis; contest was purely between the two colonial powers

### Western Sahara Advisory Opinion (1975)

- Question: was the territory terra nullius? No
- Nomadic peoples were socially and politically organized under chiefs: sufficient structure
- Court looked for “legal ties” (a deliberately loose concept) between territory and Morocco/Mauritania
- Morocco: stronger formal ties, uncontested occupation
- Mauritania: stronger cultural ties (ethnic, religious, linguistic)
- Outcome: ties existed but were not overwhelming enough to assign sovereignty
- Western Sahara declared to have the right to self-determination
- UN to conduct plebiscite
- Tension highlighted: at the decolonizing moment, the First World was softening sovereignty while the developing world was compelled into the traditional triangular state form
- No option on the ballot for “continue as we have been for thousands of years”
- Nomadic peoples’ own voices not directly consulted; court’s brief was to assess the legal situation, not determine what should happen next

### Burkina Faso and the Uti Possidetis Principle

- Principle: post-colonial state boundaries follow prior colonial administrative boundaries
- Adopted to promote stability and avoid cascading border conflicts
- Endorsed by post-colonial elites who wanted to consolidate gains from independence
- What would Palmas-style effective occupation have required instead?
- States would have had to rush to physically occupy and demonstrate control of every boundary
- Uti possidetis converts self-determination disputes into internal disputes
- Sometimes still produced civil wars and external interventions (Sudan, etc.)
- Application proved far less uniform than the principle suggests:
- El Salvador (1992): colonial administrative behavior decided the boundary
- Nicaragua: balance of effectivities
- Botswana-Namibia: imagined what the colonial power would have done
- In practice, ICJ boundary cases almost always split the difference
- Indeterminate legal vocabulary serves a legitimating function: mystifies but produces acceptable outcomes

### Territory, Force, and the Limits of International Law

- Vidmar’s claim: discovery, occupation, conquest, cession, annexation are anachronistic modes of acquiring territory
- Contemporary international law: title cannot be created or altered by historic modes
- Class pushback: this reads more as a prediction of what courts would decide, not a physical constraint
- Secession becomes illegal when it denies self-determination (widely regarded as a peremptory norm)
- Law’s power depends on the power of those who treat it as significant
- Delegitimation, diplomatic pressure, or mobilizing governments to act are the real enforcement mechanisms
- Greenland outcome: dispersed sovereignty arrangement
- US gets bases and security access; Denmark retains formal title; Greenlanders have parliament
- Negotiated by satisfying rights claims, functional interests, and symbolic/fantasy dimensions simultaneously

### Berman’s Taxonomy of International Intervention (1996)

- Three modes of public reasoning for conflicted sovereignty situations:
- Formalist: adjudicate rights and entitlements (ICJ-style weighing)
- Pragmatic: focus on material and functional interests; divide governmental tasks, set sovereignty claims aside (e.g., South China Sea resource-sharing)
- Cultural/symbolic: address the fantasy of belonging; find arrangements where all parties can feel the territory is “really theirs”
- Berman’s argument: effective international intervention must address all three layers
- Rights alone, or functions alone, are insufficient
- Jerusalem as the hard case; Greenland as a partial success
- Meta-argument: law works not as cold-blooded rational rule but by capturing the messiness and imagination of how people actually engage with the world

### Themes and Synthesis

- Law operates in a distributed, open-ended way across a wide range
- Norms become effective when treated as norms; jurisdiction becomes effective when asserted and made persuasive
- Symbolic/imaginative dimension matters: treating something as a legal entitlement creates real effects for some people
- Deference to established administrative authority is the common thread across Palmas, Uti Possidetis, and Western Sahara
- Stability over accuracy; problem-solving over principle
- Tension running through all cases: self-determination as rhetoric vs. self-determination as reality
- Vehicle of the post-colonial state was supposed to deliver self-determination but often overrode it
- Indeterminate legal vocabulary is not a failure; it is partly the mechanism by which law legitimates workable outcomes

The packet covers how territory is acquired and lost in international law, moving from classic doctrine (Palmas) through decolonisation (Western Sahara, Burkina Faso/Mali) to present-day disputes (Western Sahara after 2020, Greenland) and ending with a critique of legal frameworks themselves (Berman on Jerusalem).

### 1. Island of Palmas (United States v. Netherlands), PCA, 1928

Facts. Sole arbitrator Max Huber decided who held sovereignty over Palmas (Miangas), a small island between Mindanao (Philippines) and the Nanusa group (Dutch East Indies). The US claimed it as successor to Spain under the 1898 Treaty of Paris.

Core principles. Sovereignty means independence, meaning the exclusive right to exercise state functions over a piece of territory. Huber's key move is that holding a title at one moment is insufficient when the other side has actually exercised authority. The claimant must show sovereignty continued to exist at the critical date (here 1898). The "continuous and peaceful display of territorial sovereignty" is as good as a title. His justification is structural. Domestic law can protect abstract property rights because it has courts and enforcement. International law has no super-state, so sovereignty cannot be a purely abstract right without concrete manifestation. The intensity of display required varies with circumstances, and a remote, sparsely inhabited island needs less.

Treatment of US arguments.

- Spain could only cede what it had (nemo dat). The treaty's inclusion of the island, even without Dutch protest, added nothing.
- Discovery gives at most an inchoate title, a claim to complete sovereignty through effective occupation. It cannot defeat a definite title based on display.
- Contiguity has no foundation in international law.
- Treaty recognition (Münster 1648) was superseded by Utrecht (1714), and any evidence of possession then favours the Dutch. Spain's acquiescence after 1677 would bar it anyway.

Dutch case. Authority exercised via native vassal states and the Dutch as suzerain, with evidence from 1700 to 1906. There are gaps, but sovereignty can emerge through gradual intensification, and it did intensify before 1898. There is no evidence of any Spanish activity or any Spanish-Dutch conflict over two centuries.

Fallback reasoning. Even if Dutch evidence were insufficient, the relative strength of titles favours the Netherlands. An inchoate title built on actual state activity (flags, coats of arms, confirmed by General Wood's 1906 visit) beats an inchoate title from ancient discovery or geography. Law should protect the arrangement that actually gave inhabitants and other states some guarantee of their rights.

Holding. Palmas belongs entirely to the Netherlands.

### 2. Western Sahara Advisory Opinion, ICJ, 1975

Request. UN General Assembly Resolution 3292 (1974), which reaffirmed self-determination under Resolution 1514, asked two questions. First, was Western Sahara terra nullius when Spain colonised it (1884)? Second, if not, what legal ties did it have with Morocco and the "Mauritanian entity"? Spain was asked to postpone its planned referendum.

Question I: No. Terra nullius is a term of art tied to occupation as an original mode of acquisition. State practice of the period treated territory inhabited by socially and politically organised peoples as not terra nullius. Sovereignty there was acquired through agreements with local rulers, a derivative title. Western Sahara's peoples, though nomadic, were organised in tribes under chiefs who could represent them.

Context the Court stressed. The territory was desert, used almost entirely by nomads whose routes crossed into southern Morocco, Mauritania and Algeria. Pasture was shared, wells and burial grounds carried tribal rights, inter-tribal conflict was common, and authority lay with a sheikh subject to an assembly (Juma'a). Ties between tribes were personal (allegiance, vassalage, alliance) and tied to people rather than land.

Question II, Morocco. Morocco argued immemorial possession through centuries of public, uncontested display, effectively a Palmas-style argument. The Court said the decisive evidence is effective display at and immediately before colonisation, setting aside inferences from distant history. Result: no tie of territorial sovereignty, but evidence of allegiance between the Sultan and some nomadic tribes.

Question II, Mauritania. Mauritania conceded the Bilad Shinguitti was no state and that its statehood is not retroactive. It claimed the territory south of Sakiet El Hamra was simply part of that entity. Spain countered that a distinct Sahrawi people existed. The Court found many racial, linguistic, religious and economic ties, but the emirates and tribes were independent of each other with no common institutions, so the entity had no sovereignty. However, "legal ties" is broader than sovereignty. Rights over grazing, wells, land and burial grounds, plus inter-tribal custom, were legal ties that "knew no frontier".

Overlap. Morocco and Mauritania jointly pitched a neat picture: a Moroccan north, a Mauritanian south, some overlap, and no no-man's land. The Court rejected this. The overlap came from intersecting migration routes, the Regheibat were essentially independent, and some routes reached Algeria. The overlap shows how hard the relationships are to disentangle.

Conclusion (para. 162). Allegiance ties with Morocco, land-related rights linking the territory to the Mauritanian entity, and no tie of territorial sovereignty with either. Nothing found affects the application of Resolution 1514 and self-determination through the free expression of the territory's people.

### 3. Pérez-Peña, New York Times, 10 December 2020

A journalistic explainer on Trump's recognition of Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara. Key facts:

- Spain withdrew in 1975, Moroccan forces moved in, and Polisario declared the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic (SADR). A 16-year war ended with a 1991 ceasefire.
- Morocco holds most of the territory, including the full Atlantic coast. Polisario holds parts of the interior.
- In November 2020 Morocco moved into the UN buffer zone and Polisario repudiated the ceasefire.
- Dozens of states (mostly African) recognise the SADR, though major powers and the UN do not. Algeria hosts over 100,000 Sahrawi refugees and backs Polisario.
- A 1979 UN resolution recognised the right to self-determination and independence and deplored Moroccan occupation.
- The referendum promised in 1991 never happened. Moroccan settlement has shifted demographics, which much of the world considers illegal.
- Risks include regional war and openings for Sahel jihadist groups.
- Carnegie's Sarah Yerkes predicted damage to US-Algeria relations and suggested Biden could reverse the move.
- The recognition came packaged with Moroccan normalisation with Israel.

Update since the article. Biden did not reverse it, and things moved further. On 31 October 2025 the Security Council adopted a resolution calling Morocco's autonomy plan "the most feasible solution", by 11 votes to none with China, Russia and Pakistan abstaining and Algeria not participating. Resolution 2797 renewed MINURSO until October 2026 and omitted any reference to an independence referendum. That mandate expires this month, and I have not checked the status of its renewal.

### 4. Frontier Dispute (Burkina Faso/Mali), ICJ Chamber, 1986

Facts. Both states emerged from French West Africa (Upper Volta and French Sudan). Their Special Agreement asked for a ruling based on "the intangibility of frontiers inherited from colonization", echoing the 1964 OAU Cairo Resolution.

Uti possidetis juris.

- Nature. It is a general principle logically linked to independence wherever it occurs. It is not a regional custom of Latin America or Africa. African practice is an application of a general rule, and OAU declarations are declaratory, not constitutive.
- Purpose. It prevents "fratricidal struggles" over borders after the colonial power leaves.
- Origin. Spanish America, where the juris element gave legal title priority over effective possession, to stop outside powers claiming unexplored areas.
- Effect. It turns internal administrative lines into full international frontiers. Pre-existing international frontiers are also inherited under the general law of state succession.
- Self-determination. The Chamber admits an apparent outright conflict. It resolves it on stability grounds. African states deliberately chose uti possidetis to protect hard-won independence, and that choice shapes how self-determination is read.
- The principle applied in 1960 even though the OAU and the Cairo Resolution came later.

5. Vidmar, "Greenland and Territorial Acquisition under International Law", EJIL:Talk!, February 2025

Trigger. Trump's wish to acquire Greenland, possibly by force, which Article 2(4) of the UN Charter would plainly prohibit.

Argument.

- Discovery, occupation, conquest, cession and annexation are largely anachronistic under Charter-era rules on force and self-determination.
- Conquest and annexation (Russia in Ukraine, for example) do not shift title. Aggression breaches a peremptory norm and triggers a duty of non-recognition (Article 41 ARSIWA).
- Occupation is now an international humanitarian law concept that confers no title.
- Cession by treaty can still be lawful, but it is void if coerced or if it breaches a peremptory norm, including self-determination. Denmark therefore cannot cede Greenland without Greenlanders' consent.
- Resolution 1541's options (independence, free association, integration) apply only to Non-Self-Governing Territories. Greenland left the list in 1954 after constitutional integration into Denmark.
- The article cites Resolution 854. I believe the correct number is 849 (IX), about 75% confident.
- Greenland is therefore an internal self-determination case, governed mainly by Danish law within international limits.
- The 2009 Self-Government Act lets Greenlanders trigger independence negotiations, with a referendum and consent from both parliaments. This mirrors the Quebec Secession Reference's duty to negotiate and contrasts with Catalonia's unilateral 2017 declaration.
- The Act covers independence only, so any referendum must ask a clean independence question. After independence, joining the US would not be prima facie illegal if the people chose it. Denmark could, however, insist on a non-association clause in the independence agreement, as in the 1955 Austrian State Treaty.

### 6. Berman, "Legalizing Jerusalem or, of Law, Fantasy, and Faith" (1995–96)

Thesis. Jerusalem attracts three ultimate fantasies: full sovereignty (nationalist), internationalisation (internationalist) and spiritual guardianship (religious). Law cannot bypass these fantasies in favour of "reality". A workable settlement must accommodate them.

History. Attenuated sovereignty is roughly two centuries old. Tools include self-determination, plebiscites, minority protection, human rights, mandates, trusteeships and direct international rule (Saar, Danzig, Upper Silesia). Resolution 181 (1947) contained all of them: two states, minority guarantees, an economic union, and Jerusalem as a UN-administered corpus separatum subject to a plebiscite after ten years. Berman traces a lineage from Berlin (1878) through Upper Silesia and Resolution 181 to Dayton (1995).

Universality is contested. The Crusades, the Ottoman Capitulations and UNSCOP members wanting Christian control were all "internationalisations" serving particular interests. Zionists once used internationalist arguments (Glickson's two autonomies, Herzl's internationalised Old City), and Palestinians later did the same. Eban's "reality vs illusion" and Said's "reality vs idea" share one structure, and both fail as normative arguments. Right-wing Israeli Haetzni and left-wing Palestinian Said both compared Oslo to Vichy.

Three attitudes, each with its own fantasy.

- Formalist. Identify rights-holders and adjudicate. The fantasy is that the parties are secular legal claimants.
- Pragmatist (the dominant attitude). Parcel out functions, from mail delivery to holy sites. The fantasy is that the parties are rational interest-maximisers.
- Cultural (Berman's choice). Take passion seriously, including the policymakers' own. The Mandate system's "stages of civilisation" is his example of cultural projection disguised as form and pragmatism. Nationalist identities are also partly shaped by international legal categories.

Model solution. Adnan Abu Odeh's plan:

- East Jerusalem as al-Quds under Palestinian control.
- West Jerusalem as Yerushalaim under Israeli control.
- The walled city as "Jerusalem", with no flags and a council of Muslim, Christian and Jewish authorities.
- Each side may imagine its sovereignty extends to its own holy sites.

Its deliberate interpretive looseness is its strength. Berman calls the required stance "dédoublement passionnel", a play on Scelle's dédoublement fonctionnel, and frames it as faith requiring a "conversion experience".

Epilogue (March 1996). After Rabin's assassination and the bombings, he urges readers to reject both the assassins' monomaniacal faith and sterile rationalism.

### Threads across the packet

- Effectivités vs title. Palmas privileges effective display over paper title. Burkina Faso/Mali (juris) privileges legal title over possession. Western Sahara sits between them. Morocco ran a Palmas argument and lost, and the colonial boundary that uti possidetis would protect cuts against it.
- The legal question has moved. "Who acquired it?" (Palmas) has become "who may consent to its status?" (Western Sahara, Greenland). Vidmar treats self-determination as the master rule that disciplines even lawful cession.
- Palmas has quietly returned. Morocco has spent fifty years building exactly what Huber rewarded: administration, settlement, infrastructure and acquiescence by others. Post-Charter law says such display by an occupier cannot create title. Resolution 2797 suggests the Security Council is accepting a settlement that tracks effective control anyway. My guess, with moderate confidence (about 60%), is that this gap between doctrine and outcome is the intended discussion point of the packet.
- Greenland reverses the usual direction. Trump's rhetoric revives pre-Charter modes (purchase as cession, force as conquest). Vidmar answers with the post-Charter toolkit. The live question is whether Western Sahara shows that toolkit bending under great-power recognition.
- Berman's typology fits the other readings.
- Palmas is formalism softened by pragmatism (the interest in protecting a stable state of things).
- Burkina Faso/Mali is pragmatism stated as principle (stability over self-determination).
- The Western Sahara opinion is the closest the ICJ gets to Berman's cultural approach. It took nomadic forms of belonging seriously and refused to force them into the Moroccan and Mauritanian north/south map.
