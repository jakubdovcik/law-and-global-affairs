# Lecture 14: Strategic multilateralism, legitimacy, transnational legal process and its critics

## what Kennedy stressed

- [[wedgwood-unilateral]]: multilateralism is a strategic calculation on a 'sliding scale of legitimacy'. How you 'drape' an intervention matters; if the Council blocks, move down to NATO or coalitions. Cynical but coherent: McDougal's process realism with the sincerity stripped out.
- [[slaughter-around-un]] ('illegal but legitimate'): legitimation is retrospective, so going multilateral is a bet on the outcome (Kosovo, Iraq). The real question is **who the swing audience is**.
- **Legitimacy is a currency of power**, not a moral verdict. Delegitimation matters only if it reaches actors who can affect the action (Gaza). Smaller states (UK, Australia) have far more at stake in legality than the US, whose public is largely indifferent to UN approval.
- [[koh-tlp]]: non-traditional, non-statist, normative. Norms 'percolate upward', and North Korea can't fully exit. Written in 1995, the peak moment.
- [[goldsmith-mercer]]: the order was never as orderly, rule-based or attractive as claimed. 'Turning the Titanic toward icebergs while carefully knitting transnational legal process.' Trade elites, captured by normativity, left Midwestern workers behind.
- [[benvenisti-resilience]] and [[koh-trump]]: ignoring law is more sinister than manipulating it ('Where's Wedgwood when we need her?'). Both are defensive (Koh resists, Benvenisti archives), and **neither reimagines why law proved vulnerable**. This cohort voted more 'stabilise/restore' than any in 7–8 years.
- **Where next:**
  - a middle-power condominium of situational, issue-specific coalitions;

  - there is no robust middle-power or Chinese theorising yet.

## my notes

### Wedgwood: Strategic Multilateralism

- Multilateralism as strategic calculation, not legal obligation
- Countries choose participation based on legitimacy payoff, not normative duty
- “Sliding scale of legitimacy”: value of going multilateral depends on context
- Gyroscopic stability: informal institutions (bond markets, capital markets) also restrain states
- Multilateralism as expressive act: how you “drape” or “style” an intervention matters
- Mobilizing coalitions to request US action as cover for unilateral intent
- If Security Council blocks, move down: NATO, coalitions of the willing, regional bodies
- Hegemonic powers have more latitude to construct ad hoc multilateral cover
- Cynical but coherent: takes McDougal’s process realism further, strips sincerity

### Slaughter: Illegal but Legitimate

- 2003 op-ed: disentangles legality from legitimacy
- Legitimation is retrospective, not determined in advance
- Kosovo cited as example of action deemed legitimate after the fact
- Iraq War framing: if WMDs found and democracy followed, UN refusal would look foolish; if quagmire, US looks reckless
- Going multilateral or not is a bet on outcome
- Customary law parallel: breaking a rule to establish a new one works if it succeeds; fails if it doesn’t
- Tension in her argument
- Claims UN approval remains a “necessary component” of legitimate force use
- Hard to reconcile with endorsing strategic non-compliance
- Kosovo legitimacy questioned: retrospective legitimation depends on who the relevant audience is
- Jakub’s point: “who is the swing group?” matters; not universal acceptance

### Legitimacy as Currency of Power

- Legitimacy functions as political currency, not moral verdict
- Aberrant behavior in one context reduces political potency elsewhere (Wedgwood’s boomerang)
- Allies may withhold support; operational ease depends on perceived legitimacy
- Gaza parallel: delegitimation only matters if it affects actors who can influence the action
- Brexit reference: Johnson-era “specific and limited violation” of international law seen as political expediency
- Smaller states (UK, Australia) have far more at stake in legal normativity than the US
- US domestic public largely indifferent to UN approval; not true in many other countries
- Differential power: some states can pick and choose compliance; others cannot

### Koh: Transnational Legal Process

- Draws “transnational” from Jessup, “legal process” from 1950s American legal thought
- Three defining features:
- Non-traditional: breaks domestic/international and public/private divides
- Non-statist: sub-state actors, NGOs, administrative agents all contribute
- Normative: interaction generates and reinforces norms (“doing it reinforces doing it”)
- Compliance through repeated interaction, not top-down mandate
- Norms “percolate upward” even after setbacks; no single loss is final
- North Korea example: even outliers cannot fully exit the system without enormous cost
- Slaughter’s network theory runs parallel: FIFA, Basel banking rules, bilateral investment treaties as real sites of legalization
- Optimistic 1995 moment: post-Cold War, transnational legal order at perceived peak robustness

### Goldsmith: Critique and Disillusionment

- Rules-based order was neither as orderly, rules-based, nor normatively attractive as claimed
- Failed to prevent humanitarian debacles; didn’t account for its own failures
- Structural forces beyond Trump: China, Russia, populism, technocracy, US military exhaustion
- “Slowly turning the Titanic toward icebergs while carefully knitting transnational legal process”
- Rejects “consensus Whiggish belief” in ever-more-progressive law and institutions
- US trade complacency: rule-based system locked in 60-40 deals when raw power could have extracted more
- Left Midwestern manufacturing workers behind; elites captured by normativity of the order

### Benvenisti and the Resilience Question

- International law eroding bedrock principles; ignoring law more sinister than manipulating it
- At least Putin argued for annexation; Trump simply asserts desire without legal framing
- “Where’s Wedgwood when we need her?”: even strategic styling of legality would preserve law’s relevance
- Lawyers and historians as custodians: document and preserve law “awaiting restoration”
- Compared to Koh 2017: both defensive postures, both assume law is a static thing to protect
- Koh more active (resist); Benvenisti more archival (preserve)
- Neither reimagines law fundamentally to address why it proved vulnerable
- Class vote recalled: this cohort more “stable/restore” than any in 7-8 years; fewer foundational-change advocates

### Where Next: Middle Powers and Reimagination

- US-centric transnational legal process now under pressure from revisionist states (China, Russia) and internal populism
- Middle power condominium floated as alternative
- Situational alliances: coalitions around specific issues, not blanket alignment
- Parallels how US managed multilateralism via 1,000 different institutional forms
- Structural reform argument: current order gave great powers a “carrot” to buy in; that carrot is expiring
- Future order may need to reframe: absence of stick as the incentive, not special privilege
- Intellectual gap: no robust equivalent of American elite theorizing yet emerging from middle powers or China
- Open question for the course: which ideas from the past 60 years survive, in whose hands, and what genuinely new frameworks are needed
