# Lecture 3: Persuasion, the professional sensibility, and the porous boundary of law

## what Kennedy stressed

*Lecture of Sep 15 — distilled mainly from the class transcript.*

- **Take the court out of the picture.** The hard questions of Article 38 (how many states? do big states count more?) 'answer themselves' in advocacy: to convince Denmark you cite Danish and European practice. 'In advocacy the thing actually has legs.'
- **Every legal argument has an audience and needs uptake:** the opponent, your own population, allies, the UN, world opinion. The example: an NGO's genocide brief on Gaza moves *other* governments to act, while opposing lawyers build counter-briefs.
- **Between values and power.** 'You can't say God told me to do this … but you also can't say I have more nuclear weapons than you.' Legal argument has to sit in between, rooted partly in authority and partly in value.
- **The 'turtle'.** The system stands on a shared *professional sensibility*: 'turtles all the way down'. Its coherence is **sociological, not scientific**. Russia's Ukraine arguments 'sounded like legal arguments' to Russia; the Western profession called them bogus.
- **Consequences of the shift** (McDougal; Holmes; rights → remedies):
  - the profession matters more than judges;
  - norms become **graded** (strong and weak arguments), and their strength depends on the audience.
- **The airbase parable.** Max wants to bomb Val and needs Spain's airbases. Legality matters because a *third* decision-maker (Spain's government, its parliament, its anti-Trump public) needs a good argument. Whether anyone 'believes' doesn't matter: a good legal argument raises the political cost of resisting. **International law is 'the game of the middle powers'**; hegemons and very weak states invest less.
- **Expanding the aperture.** General principles reopen what sounds like natural law, but war, religion and empire stay excluded. 'If you put it in Latin it helps' ([[schachter-general-principles]]).
- **Jus cogens** consists of 'super-duper norms … so important we can't even say what they are' ([[ilc-fragmentation-jus-cogens]]). The categories are loose; the only real question is 'is it persuasive? can I make the argument?'
- [[charlesworth-chinkin-jus-cogens]]: jus cogens matters mostly as **symbol**, and the list reveals a background public/private divide. This links back to Berman's political/cultural split in the minority treaties.
- **A warning.** 'Turning something into an international issue where it won't be addressed very well is a way of not doing something about it' (climate: 'it needs a global solution').
- **The traditionalist counter-voice.** Widening the aperture weakens the currency. Does getting 'everybody speaking law' degrade or improve it? Kennedy leaves this open.
- **The law/non-law line goes porous in both directions:**
  - unilateral acts bind ([[nuclear-tests]]: France gained from statements that seemed binding);
  - non-binding deals work, and 'treaties … last while they last' ([[schachter-twilight]]);
  - exits multiply: Cardozo's provision-by-provision pragmatism in [[techt-v-hughes]], FDR's load-line suspension in [[lissitzyn-load-line]].
- **Two styles keep tussling, neither decisive:** one rule-bound and dogmatic about sovereignty, the other expansive and functional. That is why the casebook says 'traditionally … but increasingly'.
- **Anecdote.** In the 1980s Kennedy testified for anti-nuclear protesters (nuns who spilled blood in baby bottles on government computers) that they had a Nuremberg-based legal *duty* to act. When one said it mattered to hear 'we really do have a right', he stopped: 'if your religious beliefs aren't enough … I'm in the wrong business … it's a theater.'

## my notes

### Not force or values but something between that

- Effective persuasion
- MacDougal - move from rules that are understood scientifically to norms that are understood sociologically
- Happens at the same time as a shift from rights to remedies

### Effect of the shift

- Profession becomes more important
- Innovation in how it works, not necessarily the nature of the system

### International law is the game of middle powers

### From Sovereignty to Persuasion: The Core Shift

- Classical model: law as valid rules, binding if traceable to recognized sources (Article 38)
- Modern shift: law as persuasion, norms are real if they function, not because they are formally valid
- McDougall’s framing: law is an authoritative decision-making process, not an adjudicative one
- Disaggregates the “court” into all actors making legal arguments in all directions
- Validity replaced by effectiveness: if it persuades, it is a norm
- Analogy to Holmes: law is what courts actually decide, nothing more magisterial
- Parallel shift from rights to remedies

### Institutional Innovations: Courts and Codification

- First move: add a court to give rules an objective interpreter
- Court confined to expressing what states already agreed, keeping it positivist
- Article 38 establishes sources of law as the “rules about rules”
- Problem: customary law requires uniform practice over long time, nearly impossible to verify
- Second move: codification (Harvard Draft, ILC Restatement)
- Rules either too vague to be meaningful or too specific to generalize
- Codification stalls: states won’t agree to precise rules that constrain them

### Advocacy as the Alternative to Adjudication

- Key insight: Article 38 questions (how many states? do big states count more?) become answerable when framed as advocacy, not science
- Arguing to convince Denmark? Focus on Danish and European practice
- Norms gain traction through professional legal argument, not court validation
- Audience matters: legal arguments aimed at own population, allies, UN, world opinion, not just the opponent
- Example: Spain deciding whether to allow US use of airbases requires a legal justification for domestic political cover

### The Professional Sensibility as Foundation

- The whole system rests on a shared professional sensibility: a “turtle” holding it up
- Excludes pure force (“I have more nuclear weapons”) and pure values (“God told me”)
- Legal argument must sit between authority and value, within recognized professional vocabulary
- Russian arguments on Ukraine cited as example of arguments the Western legal profession rejected as outside the canon
- Sociological coherence, not scientific coherence, is what holds the system together
- Consequence: the profession itself becomes more significant
- Not just judges, but diplomats, NGOs, citizens, foreign ministers all participate
- Norm validity becomes a spectrum, not on/off
- Strong norms, weak norms, context-dependent norms, audience-dependent norms

### Deducing from Sovereignty: The Lotus Innovation

- Lotus case: when no rule exists, what is the baseline?
- Court deduces from the nature of sovereignty: states are free until constrained
- Restrictions on sovereignty cannot be presumed
- Territorial jurisdiction is primary; nationality is secondary
- Sovereignty becomes a generative legal principle, not just a political fact
- Spawns derived duties: good faith negotiation, prohibition on abusing rights (e.g. transboundary pollution)
- Opens a new argumentative register: “you’re violating my sovereignty” as a legal claim

### Expanding the Normative Vocabulary: General Principles and Jus Cogens

- General principles (Article 38(1)©): Lauterpacht’s project
- Draw on analogies between international and national private law (contracts, property, trespass)
- Seek principles common to all legal orders, inherent in law itself
- Then broader: principles inherent in the nature of man as a rational and social being
- Risks: sounds like natural law; cynicism acknowledged (“if you put it in Latin, it helps”)
- Jus cogens: super-norms that override even valid treaties
- Cannot be codified precisely: ILC left content to evolve through practice and jurisprudence
- Examples invoked: prohibition on genocide, torture, aggressive war
- Symbolic importance: articulates what the international community most fundamentally values
- Overlap between general principles and jus cogens is loose; categories are deliberately porous

### Feminist Critique: The Public/Private Distinction

- Charlesworth and Chinkin (early 1990s): jus cogens list reflects a public/private divide
- Prohibitions on torture and aggressive war (public, state acts) are included
- Violence against women, systematic female infanticide are not
- Structural argument: issues coded as “private” (family, culture) face double barrier
- Must pierce sovereignty and the public/private distinction to reach international law
- Minority rights framework (Berman) itself encoded this divide: political affairs to the state, cultural affairs to the minority
- Jus cogens value: not just practical uptake but symbolic articulation of community values
- Feminist project: expand the symbolic register to include women’s concerns
- Counter-tension: internationalizing an issue can be a way of not solving it
- Climate change parallel: declaring it a global problem can substitute for local action

### Blurring the Boundary: Unilateral Acts, Soft Law, and Treaty Flexibility

- Unilateral acts (Nuclear Tests case): states can bind themselves by public declarations
- France’s repeated assurances on nuclear testing held to create legal obligations
- Tension: sovereignty means freedom to change your mind vs. capacity to speak bindingly
- Non-binding agreements (Schachter): soft law often functions like hard law in practice
- Voluntary export restraints, diplomatic assurances: violated with political but not legal consequences
- “Treaties are like roses and young girls, they last while they last”
- Fundamental change of circumstances: Cardozo in Techt v. Hughes
- Effect of war on treaties is “one of the unsolved problems of international law”
- Pragmatic approach: evaluate each treaty provision on its merits, not all-or-nothing
- Roosevelt’s suspension of load-line convention for Lend-Lease: contested but unresolved
- Net result: the boundary between law and not-law becomes porous in both directions
- Things thought non-binding can acquire legal force; things thought binding can dissolve

### 1. Schachter, 'General principles and equity' (Chapter IV)

Schachter sorts general principles into five categories, each with a different source of authority:

- principles of municipal law recognised by civilised nations (Article 38(1)(c) ICJ Statute)
- principles derived from the nature of the international community
- principles intrinsic to the idea of law
- principles valid in all societies (jus rationale)
- natural justice grounded in human nature

The categories overlap, as with nemo judex in causa sua. His key claim concerns even the 'intrinsic' principles. They are established by reasoning that quietly assumes general acceptance, and they lose force once that acceptance is disputed. So in the end they rest on implied consensus.

Anti-discrimination movements, human rights and fear of nuclear war have revived natural-law universalism, and Schachter treats it as a material source of new law. Natural justice now survives inside two other headings. Its minimum standards of decency sit in human rights instruments, and its fairness and reciprocity sit in equity. He largely endorses Fitzmaurice's 1973 observation that a strong current of opinion treats natural justice as binding on states a priori, whatever their consent.

### 2. ILC Fragmentation Report (Koskenniemi, 2006), on jus cogens

The idea draws on Roman law, early modern natural law and domestic public policy. It became positive law through two VCLT articles:

- Article 53 voids any treaty that, when concluded, conflicts with a norm the international community of States as a whole accepts and recognises as non-derogable.
- Article 64 voids existing treaties when a new such norm emerges, with no retroactive effect.

The effect is strict hierarchy, since the conflicting treaty produces no legal consequences at all. The case law shows the limits. In Pinochet the House of Lords denied a former head of state immunity from extradition for torture. In Al-Adsani, however, the ECtHR accepted that torture is jus cogens and still upheld Kuwait's immunity from a civil claim in the English courts.

On content, the ILC's 2001 examples are aggression, slavery, genocide, racial discrimination and apartheid, torture, basic humanitarian law and self-determination. No authoritative list or agreed test exists. The core problem is circularity. A norm meant to limit what states may agree cannot coherently take its content from what states agree. Today's legal vocabulary also offers no plausible substitute for the religious natural law the idea grew from. Koskenniemi backs the ILC's 1966 approach, which states the effect and leaves the content to practice and tribunals.

### 3. Charlesworth and Chinkin, 'The Gender of Jus Cogens' (1993)

The authors accept that jus cogens matters mostly as a symbol, and they argue the symbolism is gendered. Its abstract, hierarchical style mirrors masculine reasoning (Gilligan), and its content mirrors what men fear from the state. The standard list in the US Restatement (Third) §702 covers genocide, slavery, disappearances, torture, prolonged arbitrary detention and systematic racial discrimination. It omits sex discrimination, which is the more widespread injustice.

The cause is the gendered public/private divide, which human rights law kept even as it challenged the old state/individual divide. The effects show up in each norm:

- Right to life. It covers killing by the state. It misses sex-selective abortion, female infanticide, unequal feeding and health care, and feminised poverty.
- Torture. The definition requires official involvement, while most violence against women happens at home. Ironically, that violence is one of the few areas of uniform state practice.
- Self-determination. It has been backed with no regard to women inside the group, as in Afghanistan after 1979 and Kuwait after 1990.

Jus cogens adds a second public/private layer, which leaves women 'doubly private'. The authors reject a separate generation of women's rights as marginalising. They want existing rights redefined across the public/private line; for example, ICCPR Article 6 could cover domestic violence. They also want jus cogens status for sexual equality, food, reproductive freedom, freedom from violence and peace. Since jus cogens is openly aspirational, they argue it can absorb these.

### 4. Nuclear Tests (Australia v France), ICJ 1974

Australia asked the Court to declare further French atmospheric tests in the Pacific unlawful and to order them stopped. France did not appear. Between June and October 1974 the French President and ministers announced that the 1974 series would be the last atmospheric tests. The Court held that these statements, taken together, bound France.

The Court's rules on unilateral declarations:

- A declaration made publicly with intent to be bound creates an obligation. No quid pro quo, acceptance or reaction is needed.
- Intent is found by interpretation, and statements limiting a state's freedom are read restrictively.
- Form is not decisive.
- Good faith, the same basis as pacta sunt servanda, entitles other states to rely on the declaration.

On the facts, the later statements had dropped an early 'in the normal course of events' hedge, and France had reserved no power to reconsider at will. France's insistence that the tests were lawful changed nothing. The claim therefore had no object, and the Court declined to rule, by nine votes to six.

### 5. Schachter, 'The Twilight Existence of Nonbinding International Agreements' (1977)

Schachter writes in response to the 1975 Helsinki Final Act. An agreement binds only if the parties intend to create legal rights and obligations. Governments rarely label instruments non-binding, so intent is inferred from wording and context, and vague statements of aims are presumed non-binding. Precise commitments can be non-binding too. Examples are gentlemen's agreements such as the 1908 US-Japan immigration understanding (honoured until 1924) and the unwritten 1946 deal on Security Council seats. Reuter argued such deals are personal to the negotiators; Schachter calls that strained and accepts that governments may legitimately commit without legal effect.

Lacking legal effect means two things. Breach creates no legal responsibility, which Schachter treats as an empirical finding from state practice. The agreement is also not governed by international law, which is why the VCLT excludes it. Legal consequences can still follow, chiefly estoppel. A party could be stopped from challenging an election held under a seat-sharing deal that others relied on. While such agreements last, they can be authoritative, and they are often achievable where a treaty would fail. Their political force deserves reinforcement, as monitoring of Helsinki's human rights provisions was already showing.

### 6. VCLT Articles 61 and 62

- Article 61 (impossibility). A party may terminate or withdraw if performance becomes impossible because an object indispensable to the treaty has permanently disappeared or been destroyed. Temporary impossibility permits only suspension.
- Article 62 (fundamental change, or rebus sic stantibus). The wording is deliberately negative. An unforeseen change may be invoked only if the original circumstances were an essential basis of consent and the change radically transforms the obligations still to be performed. It can never be invoked against a boundary treaty, and a qualifying change can justify suspension in place of termination.

Neither ground is open to a party whose own breach caused the situation.

### 7. Techt v Hughes (New York Court of Appeals, 1920, Cardozo J)

Sara Techt, born American, took her husband's Austro-Hungarian nationality on marrying him in 1911, as federal law then required. Her father died intestate in December 1917, 20 days after the US declared war on Austria-Hungary. As an enemy alien she could not inherit land under New York law. Her claim therefore turned on whether an 1848 US-Austria treaty protecting alien heirs survived the war.

Cardozo treats the question pragmatically:

- Treaties of alliance fall, and dispositive treaties such as boundary and cession treaties survive.
- The governing standard is that provisions compatible with hostilities continue unless expressly terminated.
- Termination is for the political branches, through denunciation, contrary legislation or peace treaties.
- Until they act, courts presume what the government intended, provision by provision. A treaty falls as a whole only if it is indivisible.

The US let peaceful enemy aliens buy and hold property, so inheritance offended no war policy, and Techt inherited. Cardozo admits the field gives judges few precepts. He rests his choice on legal tradition, statutory policy, fair dealing and national honour.

### 8. The Load Line case (Lissitzyn 1967; Briggs 1942)

In August 1941 the US, still a non-belligerent, suspended the 1930 International Load Line Convention for the duration of the emergency. Roosevelt and Acting Attorney General Biddle argued that:

- the treaty assumed normal peacetime shipping, and war had destroyed that assumption;
- rebus sic stantibus therefore gave an unquestioned right to suspend unilaterally;
- consulting the other parties was a matter of procedure that did not affect that right.

Eight Latin American parties consented, and no protest is recorded. Briggs objected on four points:

- States invoking the doctrine ground it in the parties' original intention, which Biddle never showed for a safety convention.
- Customary law, per Chesney Hill, bars unilateral termination and requires agreement of the parties or an international authority. Dropping that element leaves a bare plea of state necessity.
- He saw no real necessity for the suspension.
- Biddle's authorities did not support him, and one was an edition of Oppenheim not yet published.

### How it fits together

The pack maps the edge of state consent from three sides:

- Above consent. Norms said to bind regardless of consent keep sliding back into it, through Schachter's implied consensus, Article 53's reliance on acceptance by States, and Koskenniemi's circularity.
- Below treaty level. Commitments such as declarations and gentlemen's agreements gain force through good faith, reliance and estoppel.
- Exit routes. Impossibility, fundamental change and war are all policed by presumed original intention (Article 62, Cardozo, Briggs).

My reading, at about 70% confidence, is that good faith and reliance do more real work in this topic than hierarchy. Nuclear Tests is the awkward case. The Court found an intent to be bound in a government that denied owing anything. I suspect (about 60%) this was a way around ruling on legality. Four judges in a joint dissent said the Court had misread a claim that sought exactly that ruling.

### What happened next

- Jus cogens and sex discrimination. The ILC's 2022 draft conclusions on peremptory norms annex a non-exhaustive list: aggression, genocide, crimes against humanity, basic humanitarian law, racial discrimination and apartheid, slavery, torture and self-determination. Sex discrimination is still missing (90% confident).
- Gender-based violence took another route. It advanced through ordinary law, via state due diligence (Opuz v Turkey, ECtHR 2009) and CEDAW General Recommendation 35 (2017), which treats the prohibition as customary (85%). So the feminist agenda moved forward largely without jus cogens. That weakens Charlesworth and Chinkin's focus on the symbolic tier (my judgement, 60%).
- Techt as evidence for their thesis. Sara Techt lost her citizenship under the Expatriation Act of 1907, which the Cable Act of 1922 largely reversed (90%). A woman's inheritance thus hinged on her husband's nationality and a war she had no part in.
- Unilateral declarations were narrowed. In Frontier Dispute (Burkina Faso/Mali, 1986), a presidential statement did not bind Mali because a formal agreement was available. In 1995 the Court dismissed New Zealand's challenge to France's resumed underground tests, because the 1974 undertaking covered atmospheric tests only (85%).
- Briggs won the argument. VCLT Articles 65 to 67 now require notification, a waiting period and dispute settlement (90%). In Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros (Hungary/Slovakia, 1997), the ICJ:
- treated Articles 60 to 62 as largely customary;
- found that political and economic change and new environmental knowledge fell short of a fundamental change;
- confined the plea to exceptional cases;
- ruled that necessity at most excuses non-performance while the treaty stays in force (85%).
- Non-binding agreements are now routine. The US framed the 2015 JCPOA as political commitments. When it withdrew in 2018, Iran's ICJ case rested on the 1955 Treaty of Amity instead (85%).

### Two errors in the pack

- Paragraph 365 of the ILC report calls 'article 31 VCLT' a mere priority rule. Article 31 is the interpretation rule. The rule on successive treaties is Article 30, and the other likely intended reference is Article 103 of the UN Charter.
- The pack links the Al-Adsani immunity finding to ECHR Articles 1 and 3. The immunity holding itself came under Article 6(1), by nine votes to eight (85%).
