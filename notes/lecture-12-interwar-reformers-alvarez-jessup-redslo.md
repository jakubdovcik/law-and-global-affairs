## what Kennedy stressed

- [[alvarez-new-il]] (1929):
  - against an 'exclusively juridical science'; law rebuilt from politics, history, public opinion and morality;
  - a three-tier rule structure; sovereignty as interactive (the French social school);
  - educate the masses;
  - the speech nearly ended his career. Critique: elitist and blind to power.
- [[jessup-functional]]: organise law by **function and problem**, not inherited categories, because a leasehold and a transcontinental railroad differ. He busts open the triangle (agencies, corporations, NGOs) and already sees a stack of legalities in the 1920s. Critique: who defines the problem? 'A square is the shape a library wants to be.' (The Jessup moot is ironically formalist.)
- [[redslob-nationalities]]: each era has a ruling principle, and today's is nationality, defined psychosocially. It collides with territorial, geographic and economic viability. The resolution is adjusting psychology through minority rights, which is deeply interventionist and written from the dominant group's view.
- **What the three share:** none treats sovereignty as absolute, and all are **blind to power**.
- [[vagts-third-reich]]:
  - a revisionist power *uses* the legal vocabulary; Geneva rules were applied differently West and East, continuous with Lawrence;
  - rebus sic stantibus was used to exit treaties;
  - **the structural failure was universities and the profession becoming arms of the state**. Once the legal community lost its autonomy, it lost its moderating function;
  - the question for government lawyers: what do you owe when policy shifts under you?
- **The limit of the modernist imagination** is that it assumes convergent values. 'Very few societies think what they are doing is unethical.' Revisionist challenges keep recurring (socialism, Nazism, decolonisation, post-1989), and **'what does international law offer when values genuinely diverge?'**

## my notes

### Course Arc and Session Context

- Session situates reformers of early 20th-century international law: what they proposed, what succeeded, what failed
- Three thinkers examined: Alejandro Alvarez, Philip Jessup, and Redslob
- Nazi international law covered as a case study in disruptive revisionism
- Next week: post-war American imagination of international order

### Alvarez: Social Interdependence and the New International Law

- 1929 speech to the Grotius Society; Chilean diplomat-lawyer, trained in France, later ICJ judge
- Against the old “exclusively juridical science”: rules deduced in isolation from society, politics, history
- Not abolishing law, but reconstructing it around a richer set of inputs:
- Politics and international relations
- History and social context
- Public opinion and popular psychology
- Morality, values, equity
- Codification as modernization: existing rules were unknowable; new rules must be articulated clearly
- Three-tier rule structure proposed:
- Universal rules for fundamental matters
- General rules followed by most states
- Regional or special rules for two or more nations
- Sovereignty reframed: not absolute and individualistic, but interactive and reciprocal
- Draws on French social school in private law (shift from laissez-faire individualism to social solidarity)
- Sovereigns in community, not drifting autonomously
- Public education as strategy: international lawyers must speak to the masses, not just diplomats
- “So long as the international spirit is not inculcated in the masses, no respect for legal rules is possible”
- Speech was scandalous at the time; nearly destroyed his career
- Critique: elitist cosmopolitan vision; ignores power dynamics; assumes elite consensus can deliver ethical order

### Jessup: Functionalism and Problem-Oriented Law

- Different strand from Alvarez: oriented around the word “function,” not social interdependence
- Against organizing law by inherited conceptual categories (e.g., all treaties in one bucket)
- Treaties cover security alliances, trade, administration, judicial procedure: vastly different functions
- Same problem in municipal law: property rules for a leasehold vs. a transcontinental railroad should differ
- Core move: get law out of its categories and into the space of problems
- Start with what shippers, bankers, migrants actually need; design rules fit for those purposes
- Experts in each domain, not abstract jurists, should inform rule-making
- Breaks from Alvarez: not deducing from the nature of sovereignty or interdependence; starts with concrete situations
- “States as triangles connected only at their peak”: Jessup wants to bust open the triangle
- Relevant actors include administrative agencies, corporations, NGOs, not just states
- Already envisioning a stack of legalities (national regulatory + international) in the 1920s
- Practical legacy: problem-oriented casebooks; UCC codification followed similar logic (rules shaped by business practice)
- Critique: who decides what the problem is? Experts (shippers, bankers) carry their own interests and power
- “A square is the shape a library wants to be”: functional reasoning can mask normative choices
- Jessup competition named after him, but ironically runs as a strict formalist moot

### Redslob: The Method of Principles and the Nationality Problem

- 1931 piece on the problem of nationalities; lives in Strasbourg, writes from within the post-WWI minority crisis
- Method: each historical era has a predominant legal principle; current era’s is nationality
- “Every nation should have its state; every state should comprise its nation”
- Problem: what is a nation? Race, language, religion all fail as definitions
- Lands on a psychosocial definition: nationalism as a felt, collective psychological experience
- Principle of nationality collides with other principles:
- State territorial cohesion and natural frontiers
- Geographic viability and access to the sea
- Economic viability
- Resolution: not by weighing principles against each other, but by adjusting the psychological basis of conflict
- Minority rights as the tool: if minorities can be assimilated into the state’s identity, the conflict dissolves
- Dismisses secession (too radical) and population exchange (ethnic cleansing, only for extreme cases)
- Critique: deeply interventionist; assumes minorities can and should assimilate
- Ignores power asymmetry: framework written from the perspective of historically dominant groups
- Examples he cites (Subcarpathian Ruthenia, Baltic minorities) mostly did not work out
- Workable only where existing power structures permitted it, not as a principled solution

### Comparing the Three Reformers

- All three depart from sovereignty as absolute and individualistic
- Alvarez: sovereigns in interdependent community
- Jessup: sovereignty largely irrelevant; states are instrumentalities for solving problems
- Redslob: states are intermediaries between international capacity and psychosocial problems in society
- Alvarez and Jessup often hard to distinguish in practice; both want law connected to social reality
- Alvarez goal: ethical, peaceful international society; Jessup goal: functional problem-solving
- Redslob is the most interventionist: reconstructing national identities, not just adjusting rules
- Shared blind spot: power dynamics largely absent from all three frameworks

### Nazi International Law: Disruption and Legitimation

- Included as a case study in what a revisionist power does with international law
- Nazis used international law selectively for legitimation: Lebensraum, racial hierarchy, sovereign inequality
- Continuity with Lawrence’s civilizational framework: differentiated application of rules by race/region
- Geneva Conventions applied differently in the West vs. the East
- Surprising finding: international law remained relevant even as Germany grew more powerful
- Early phase: Germany weak, emphasized sovereign equality
- Later phase: stronger, but still engaged with legal vocabulary rather than discarding it
- Rebus sic stantibus invoked to exit inconvenient treaties (as did the Soviets with tsarist-era agreements)
- Key structural failure: universities and legal community became arms of the state
- Loss of autonomy between legal community and state = loss of moderating function
- Contrast with Alvarez’s cosmopolitan college ideal: independence is what makes that vision work
- Raises the standing question for government lawyers: what is your obligation when state foreign policy shifts dramatically under you?

### Pluralism, Revisionism, and the Limits of the Modernist Imagination

- All three reformers assume a convergent community of values; none seriously grapples with value pluralism
- Nazi case makes the problem vivid: revisionist regimes assert an alternative ethical framework, not an absence of ethics
- “Very few societies have the idea that what we are doing is unethical”
- Recurring pattern across 20th century: socialist revolution, Nazism, decolonization, post-Soviet moment
- Each time, a disruptive power challenges the inherited order
- Each time, the system eventually absorbed or outlasted the challenge
- Post-1989 parallel: “end of history” moment reprised the Alvarez cosmopolitan fantasy
- Books on democratic governance as universal principle; China joining WTO as convergence
- Didn’t last long
- Open question the course is building toward: what does international law offer when values genuinely diverge?
