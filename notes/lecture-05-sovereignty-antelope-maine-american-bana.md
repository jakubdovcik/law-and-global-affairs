# Lecture 5: Sovereignty: Antelope, Maine, American Banana, Cohen, Álvarez, Knop

## what Kennedy stressed

- **'Territoriality is weak in the face of property rights'**, and there are hierarchies among sovereigns. Statehood is contested terrain across humanitarian organisations, sanctions regimes, courts and agencies (Cuba).
- [[antelope]]: the no-norm gap (as in Lotus) is filled by **transnational property** travelling with its owner. Territorial jurisdiction is never seriously invoked, and sovereign power reinforces private rights in transit.
- **The pattern across** [[schooner-exchange]], [[antelope]] and [[american-banana]]: the relation between private entitlement and public power is **unstable and historically shifting** ([[public-private]]).
- [[maine-kathiawar]]:
  - divisible sovereignty and 'dependent sovereigns';
  - a 'disorder' doctrine (sovereignty 'in abeyance') that prefigures failed-state and humanitarian-intervention arguments. Britain caused the famine that produced the disorder;
  - indirect rule's local/external split runs on into minority regimes and gender carve-outs.
- **Holmes the pragmatist** consolidates the most formalist, territorially absolute sovereignty. He ignored the obvious jurisdictional hooks (a New Jersey conspiracy, nationality, effects).
- [[cohen-property-sovereignty]]: sovereignty is a relation *between sovereigns about* territory. Property is delegated sovereignty, and a 6% return works like a tax. This is the bridge back to Maine.
- Álvarez ([[corfu-1949]]): sovereignty as 'an international social function'. **Recognition is a social practice**, neither declaratory nor constitutive: 'the process itself produces the authority'.
- [[knop-feminism-sovereignty]]: a friendly critic of first-generation feminism who finds an unlikely resource in McDougal's process theory. This is 1993 end-of-century optimism; next comes 'what happened to that dream'.

### States, Recognition, and the Statehood Problem

- States remain central actors in international law, though relativized by other players
- Statehood criteria raise the norm/procedure problem: who applies the criteria, and with what effect?
- Montevideo Convention: Latin American project asserting all states are legally equal
- Recognition: political or legal obligation?
- Declaratory vs. constitutive debate
- Effects vary by who is recognizing: State Dept., courts, international institutions, corporations
- From the recognized state’s perspective, recognition is effectively constitutive of its external face
- Cuba as example: statehood is a contested terrain across humanitarian orgs, sanctions regimes, foreign courts, and administrative agencies

### The Antelope (1825): Slavery, Property, and the Law of Nations

- Core question: does the US abolition of the slave trade constitute a law of nations norm binding on all states?
- Marshall’s answer: no unity of practice, so no binding international norm
- Spain, Portugal, and many others still actively engaged in the trade
- Africa invoked as a region where war-based enslavement persisted under a different legal order
- Hints at international law not being a universal unity, foreshadowing the civilized/uncivilized distinction
- No-norm situation (like Lotus): what fills the gap?
- Marshall falls back on transnational property rights
- Property established legally elsewhere travels with the owner; territorial sovereign yields to it
- Outcome: slaves divided by claimed nationality
- American-ship slaves freed; Spanish-claimed slaves returned
- Portuguese claim failed for lack of proof; no owner came forward after 5 years
- Remainder allocated by lot, minus casualties
- Privateer context: the capturing vessel likely an American ship flying a Portuguese flag to evade US slave trade prohibition
- Key tension: territorial jurisdiction never seriously invoked
- Property rights trump territorial authority
- Sovereign power understood primarily as reinforcing and consolidating private rights in transit

### Schooner Exchange vs. Antelope: Public vs. Private Rights

- Schooner: private citizens subject to territorial jurisdiction; public ship immune via implied consent
- Antelope: private property rights travel and override territorial sovereign authority
- American Banana (Holmes, 1909): private rights extinguished by the territorial sovereign where the act occurred
- Pattern across cases: the relationship between private entitlement and public power is unstable and historically shifting

### Maine and Divisible Sovereignty in Colonial India

- Maine writing as colonial legal officer in London, mid-19th century
- Key claim: sovereignty is divisible; princely states are sovereign but not independent
- “Dependent sovereigns”: local authority over internal matters, British supremacy over external affairs, trade, and military intervention
- Context: East India Company rule transferred to the Crown in 1858; indirect rule chosen to avoid full fiscal and administrative responsibility
- Disorder doctrine: if a local sovereign fails to maintain order, the supreme power may intervene without abrogating local sovereignty
- Sovereignty placed “in abeyance,” not extinguished
- Analogy to modern “failed state” and humanitarian intervention arguments
- Maine acknowledges British governance caused the famine that created the disorder justifying intervention
- Indirect rule extended globally: Islamic imams given secular authority in Northern Nigeria; local rulers created or empowered across Southeast Asia
- Legacy: division between “local” (family, religion, criminal) and “external” (trade, foreign affairs) domains
- Continuity into minority protection regimes and, later, gender-related jurisdictional carve-outs

### Holmes and Territorial Absolutism (American Banana, 1909)

- Facts: United Fruit allegedly conspired with Costa Rica to seize American Banana’s plantation in disputed Panama/Costa Rica territory
- Holmes’s two moves:
- Sovereignty is territorial and limits US legislative reach to US territory
- Sovereignty is a question of fact: de facto military control is sufficient, regardless of de jure title
- Possible jurisdictional hooks Holmes ignored:
- Conspiracy decisions potentially made in the US (New Jersey)
- Nationality of both plaintiff and defendant
- Effects felt by American consumers (effects doctrine)
- Holmes as legal realist: pragmatist by disposition, yet this opinion consolidates the most formalist, territorially absolute conception of sovereignty
- This does not remain good law: US and EU antitrust jurisdiction later extended extraterritorially

### Cohen: Property as Sovereignty, Sovereignty as Relation

- Property is not a person-thing relationship; it is a relationship between people about a thing
- “Bundle of rights”: duties, privileges, powers distributed among owner and non-owners
- Implication for sovereignty: sovereignty is not a prince-territory relationship; it is a relationship between sovereigns about a territory
- Entitlements and obligations are relational, not absolute
- Property as delegated sovereign power: when a state recognizes property rights, it grants a form of sovereignty to private individuals
- The 6% corporate return analogy: functionally equivalent to a tax, i.e., a sovereign extraction
- Domestic critique: property law creates hierarchical authority analogous to feudalism
- Many propertyless workers must compete for access; landlords hold structural bargaining power
- Undermines laissez-faire “freedom of contract” narrative
- International transposition: if property is state-created and relational, territorial sovereigns can reorganize it; foreign property rights are a qualification on, not an override of, territorial sovereignty
- Cohen as bridge back to Maine: sovereignty is divisible and relational, not the Holmes absolute

### Alvarez in Corfu Channel: Sovereignty as Social Institution

- Alvarez’s definition: sovereignty = the whole body of rights and attributes a state possesses in its territory and in its relations with other states
- Key departure from classical view:
- Sovereignty is no longer an absolute individual right
- States are bound by rules they did not explicitly consent to, due to social interdependence
- “Sovereignty is an international social function of a psychological character”
- Connects to Lotus debate: entitlements and duties can be deduced from the nature of sovereignty
- Recognition and statehood as social practice: neither purely declaratory nor purely constitutive
- Courts, agencies, corporations all recognize for different purposes with different mindsets
- The process itself produces the authority

### Feminist Critique and the Distributed Sovereignty Model (Knapp)

- Knapp: friendly critic of first-generation international legal feminism (Charlesworth et al.)
- First-gen critique: sovereignty is a male totalizing structure; dismantle it
- Knapp’s response: too simple; sometimes women need empowered states to enforce protections
- McDougall’s process theory as unlikely feminist resource:
- International law as a continuous flow of transnational interactions among distributed actors
- Authority = combination of normative claim and actual control, not pure fact or pure norm
- Elshtain’s overlapping sovereignties vision: multiple institutional systems, each valid in its domain, none absolute, most operating without serious conflict
- Knapp’s argument: progressive feminism must engage the post-Corfu interactive model, not the Holmes-era absolute sovereignty straw man
- End-of-century optimism (circa 1993):
- Distributed sovereignty seen as opening for human rights, humanitarian intervention, minority protection
- Human rights vocabulary could enter the disaggregated decision process through many actors
- Hegemonic backing of US and Europe made the dream seem viable
